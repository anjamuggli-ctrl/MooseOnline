Pedagogy · Topic

Leading learning conversations

How do conversations move learning forward? Theories, conversation formats, models, tensions and options for action in practice.

What it’s about

Teachers talk with students all the time: briefly in passing, in coaching conversations, when giving feedback on work and in progress reviews with parents. Learning conversations are the ones in which learning itself becomes the topic: Where are you now, where do you want to go, and what is your next step?

This page first clarifies what makes a learning conversation, which formats and feedback levels exist, and which stance and language matter. It then shows how conversations strengthen self-efficacy, learning strategies and the way students handle mistakes, and presents models for practice, from GROW to solution-focused questions and “Ich schaffs” to progress reviews. You can work through it from top to bottom or jump around using the table of contents.

Getting started

Starting point: three sentences, three conversations

“Good job!”
“You just need to practice more.”
“Any questions? No? Good.”

Sentences like these are said every day, often in passing. They are meant kindly, but they say little about what went well, how to move on and who plans the next step. This page asks: How do I talk with students about learning so that they keep thinking for themselves, trust in their abilities and know what to do next?

Line of thought · three questions for every learning conversation
1
Who is talking? How much room do students have for their view, their questions and their ideas? → Dialogue
2
About what? About the task, the learning process, the regulation of learning, or the person? → Levels
3
Where to? Does the conversation end with a concrete next step the student has chosen? → Models

Guiding idea

A learning conversation is a conversation about a person’s learning, held with that person. It makes their current level and progress visible, clarifies expectations and prompts them to think about their own thinking. The teacher is responsible for a good course of the conversation. Students remain the experts on their own learning and have a say in how it continues.

A · Basic understanding

Conversation, dialogue, relationship

Not every conversation at school is a dialogue. Often the point is to pass on information as accurately as possible. A learning conversation aims for more: the participants should come to understand something together that neither of them fully knew beforehand, namely how this person learns and how things can move forward.

Communication

Information is passed from one person to another as accurately as possible, such as instructions or a date. In the classroom this is often necessary and useful.

Dialogue

Both listen to each other freely and without prejudice and in doing so create something new that both make their own. Neither tries to sell the other their own truth.

Distinction by David Bohm, cited in Juul & Jensen, 2019, p. 147.

Unequal roles
The relationship between teacher and students is asymmetrical: the teacher has more power and more responsibility. Equality is therefore not possible, but equal dignity is. The adult is responsible for the quality of the conversation.Juul & Jensen, 2019, pp. 146–148
Who is responsible for what?
In coaching, the teacher is responsible for the process (setting, structure, questions, appreciation), the student for the content (topic, goal, solution, next step).Kremers & Schlüter, 2018, pp. 9–10
Conversation and relationship
Every conversation has a content aspect and a relationship aspect (Watzlawick). Juul and Jensen put it this way: the quality of relationships rises with the quality of conversations.Bamberger, 2022, p. 69; Juul & Jensen, 2019, p. 149
Tension · your own intention
Teachers almost always pursue a goal in conversations that goes beyond the conversation itself. That is legitimate. It becomes problematic when the intention stays hidden (“You sense the intention and you are put off,” Goethe). Juul and Jensen advise stating your intention openly while staying curious about the other person’s view:
“I’d like to talk with you, Thomas, because I don’t like the way you treat the others. But I don’t know how you see it. That’s why I hope we can talk about it.” – “I’m only mean because they always tease me …” – “They tease you? I didn’t realize that. Can you tell me more?”Juul & Jensen, 2019, pp. 149–150 (example shortened)
A · Conversation formats

From feedback in passing to the progress review

Learning conversations differ in length, occasion, participants and scope. Lötscher, Naas and Roos describe three levels at which assessments are communicated: in class, in conversations with students and parents, and at the institutional level in the report card (2023, pp. 106–107).

FormatOccasion and lengthWhat it is aboutTypical questions
Feedback talkclassroom levelspontaneous or planned, often during work phases, a few minutesa specific task, the solution path, the next step“How did you go about it? Where do you want to go? What will you do next?”
Learning or coaching conversationclassroom levelarranged individually from time to time, 15–45 minuteslooking back on a learning period, goals and plans, interests, bigger difficulties with learning or motivation“What is your concern? What have you already tried? What should be achieved by the end?”
Assessment conversation or progress reviewschool and parentsat the end of a semester or school year, with the child and parents or guardianslearning progress, self- and external assessment, support agreement, educational pathway“Which piece of work shows your progress? What do you plan to do?”
Report cardinstitutional levelend of semester or yeara statement, condensed into grades, about reaching the learning goalsnot a conversation, but a reason for one

Not all counseling is the same
  • Expert counseling (mentoring): an experienced person gives advice and support.
  • Coaching (process counseling): the counselor helps sharpen the person’s own goals and develop their own solutions. Advice comes at the very end, if at all, and only on request.
  • Learning coaching: a special form in which knowledge about learning and study techniques may also be offered.
  • Limit: coaching is not psychotherapy.
Kremers & Schlüter, 2018, pp. 12–13, 16–20, 80–81
Line of thought · clarifying roles
The same teacher counsels, teaches and grades. Kremers and Schlüter recommend keeping counseling and grading as separate as possible (p. 24). Where that is not possible, transparency helps: say at the beginning whether the conversation will be graded and what will happen with what is said. Juul and Jensen also distinguish conversations that are mandatory, spontaneous, planned or necessary (2019, p. 146). The clearer the setting, the more open the conversation (own synthesis).

A · Levels

What are we talking about? Four levels of feedback

Feedback is among the most effective factors for learning success; in Hattie’s synthesis it reaches an effect size of d = 0.75 (Wisniewski & Zierer, 2017, p. 25). What matters, however, is not the amount but the quality, and part of that is the question of which level the feedback addresses (pp. 26–31, after Hattie & Timperley, 2007).

Task

How well was the task done and understood? What is right, what is wrong?

Process

How was the work done? Which strategies, which careless mistakes, what pace?

Self-regulation

How did the person steer their learning: attention, time, monitoring?

Self

Evaluation of the person: “You’re awesome!”, “You’re a hard-working student.”

Which feedback occurs in class

Own illustration of the ranges from three studies (Hattie & Masters, 2011; Van den Bergh et al., 2010; Gan, 2011), after Wisniewski & Zierer, 2017, p. 33.

Finding
Students get the feedback they need most least often: feedback on self-regulation makes up only about 1–2% of feedback in class. When teachers are asked which feedback they themselves would like after a classroom observation, the vast majority choose exactly this level.Wisniewski & Zierer, 2017, pp. 32–33
The “self” level
Praise and blame directed at the person contain no information about learning and have little effect, sometimes even a negative one: those who are praised don’t want to risk their positive image, and frequent praise creates dependence. For the relationship, feedback on the person can still be valuable. The guiding principle is “less is more.”Wisniewski & Zierer, 2017, pp. 29–31; Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 109
Novices and advanced learners
Those new to a field mainly need feedback on the task: how is a child supposed to know that 3 + 6 = 8 is wrong? Those who already know a lot usually know themselves what went wrong and need help with self-regulation. Best for everyone is complete feedback that connects the three learning levels.Wisniewski & Zierer, 2017, pp. 34–36
Three directions
Feed back: How am I doing, what has been added since last time?
Feed up: Where am I going, where do I stand compared with the goal?
Feed forward: What is the next step?
In a conversation these questions are not worked through rigidly but used like a spotlight, depending on the situation.Lötscher et al., 2023, pp. 110–111; Wisniewski & Zierer, 2017, pp. 36–38

A · Ways of speaking

Five ways of speaking and personal language

Juul and Jensen distinguish five ways of speaking. Each has its place. For relationships and for conversations about personal concerns, however, only one works: personal language (2019, pp. 151–157).

social

polite, general; regulates distance, ideal for small talk

academic

analyzes, forms hypotheses, aims for objectivity

professional

technical terms with a clear meaning; provides certainty

pedagogical

judging, suggestive, advising; from matter-of-fact to manipulative

personal

your own thoughts, values and feelings, authentic: “I would like …”, “I think …”
Personal is not private
Personal language shows where I stand as a person on something. Private stories from my own life are something else and do not replace personal contact. An “I” at the beginning of a sentence does not make a judgment personal: “I really can’t see that you’re interested” is still a judgment. And words are only credible when body language and voice match them.Juul & Jensen, 2019, pp. 155–158
Practice · helping students find personal language
Adolescents often play down serious concerns. Adults can offer them words:
  • “You look like you don’t agree. Is that right?”
  • “I’d rather have an honest no than a half-hearted yes.”
Juul & Jensen, 2019, pp. 151, 161

A · Stance

Equal in dignity, appreciative, solution-focused

Conversation techniques only work if the stance behind them is right. Three sources describe this stance from different directions but reach similar conclusions.

Equal dignity

Everyone deserves equal respect, even with different status. The views, needs, feelings and self-images of everyone involved have the same priority. No one is dismissed because their feelings are considered “childish” or “unrealistic.”Juul & Jensen, 2019, pp. 171–172

Recognition rather than praise

Recognition means accepting a child’s inner reality and self-image as equally valid, with openness and curiosity. Praise and criticism strengthen self-confidence (“What can I do?”), recognition strengthens self-esteem (“Who am I?”).Juul & Jensen, 2019, pp. 74–77, 175–177

Solution focus

Students are experts on their own lives and have resources. Problems are normal and not always present. A solution means doing more of what works and trying something else when something doesn’t work.de Shazer & Dolan, 2008, after Bamberger, 2022, pp. 11–12
Assumptions “until proven otherwise”
The WOWW approach assumes that all students want their parents and teachers to be proud of them, that they want to learn new things, make choices, say what they think and belong to a group. It also assumes that teachers want to be good teachers and parents want to be good parents. Such assumptions show in the language we use.Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 15–16
Connection · praise under the microscope
Three sources, one direction: praise directed at the person has hardly any effect on learning (Wisniewski & Zierer), it can encourage unfavorable attributions (Lötscher et al.), and it does not replace recognition (Juul & Jensen). More effective are specific compliments about what someone has done, as used in the WOWW approach (Berg & Shilts, 2005, p. 32), combined with information on the task, process or self-regulation (own synthesis).
Practice · turning praise into information
  • instead of “Great!” → “You simplified the equation first. That made the rest much easier.”
  • instead of “You’re so talented.” → “You tried three ways until one worked.”
  • instead of “Well done!” → “I was impressed by how calmly you explained it to the class.”
own examples after Lötscher et al., 2023, pp. 109–111

B · Motivation

Building self-efficacy: “I can do this”

Self-efficacy is the belief that one can carry out the actions needed to reach a goal (Bandura). It is among the best predictors of later educational success at the secondary level, and it is reciprocally related to performance: good performance strengthens self-efficacy, and high self-efficacy contributes to good performance (Lefrançois, 2015, p. 339).

Four sources according to Bandura
  • Own successes: only have an effect if they are attributed to something one can influence, not to luck or an easy task.
  • Role models: comparisons with peers are the most useful.
  • Encouragement: it is easier to maintain self-efficacy when others who matter to us express their confidence in our abilities.
  • Feelings: nervousness can be interpreted as an obstacle or as energy.
Lefrançois, 2015, pp. 340–341 (after Bandura, 1986, 1997)
Explanations that help
It is favorable when success is attributed to one’s own effort or strategy, and failure to a lack of effort or to the difficulty of the task. Explanations such as talent or chance are unfavorable because they cannot be changed. Teachers therefore pay attention to sentences like “I can’t do it anyway.”Lötscher et al., 2023, pp. 108–109 (after Dweck, 2010)
Connection · self-concept and comparison
Whether someone believes they can do something also depends on what they compare themselves with. Feedback based on their own progress (individual reference norm) particularly strengthens weaker students. More on the page Analyzing learning.Möller & Trautwein, 2020, pp. 202–203
Practice · using the four sources in conversation
Make successes visible: “Show me a task you couldn’t do a month ago. What did you do to get there?”
Choose close role models: “Who in your class also found this hard at one point? How did she learn it?”
Give reasons for your confidence: “I think you can do this because last week you stuck with the equation, too.”
Reframe nervousness: “Your pounding heart shows that this matters to you. What helps you use that energy?”
own examples for the sources after Lefrançois, 2015; on expressing confidence see also Furman & Ahola, 2017, p. 116, and Bauer & Hegemann, 2013, steps 7–8
B · Learning strategies

Discussing learning strategies in conversation

Learning strategies are activities aimed at a learning or retention goal that go beyond the bare minimum (Hasselhorn & Gold, 2022, p. 89). A learning conversation is a good place to make them conscious, because many students use strategies without thinking about them, or they know a strategy and still don’t use it.

cognitive

rehearsing and memorizing, organizing (sorting, summarizing), elaborating (linking to prior knowledge, creating your own examples)

metacognitive

planning, monitoring, checking your own understanding, adjusting your approach

resource management

organizing the learning environment, time, workspace, materials and study groups

Own illustration after Hasselhorn & Gold, 2022, pp. 89–90. Keller, 2015, ch. 5, describes a basic repertoire of general strategies (see Learning prerequisites).

The motivation dip

Own illustration after Hasselhorn & Gold, 2022, fig. 2.8, p. 97 (after Miller & Seier, 1994).

How strategies are acquired
1
Mediation deficiency: the strategy cannot be used even after it has been demonstrated (mainly in very young children).
2
Production deficiency: the strategy is only used when prompted and soon abandoned because the benefit is not convincing.
3
Utilization deficiency: the strategy is used spontaneously but does not yet pay off because it is not automatic.
Hasselhorn & Gold, 2022, pp. 95–97. A repertoire that can be used flexibly often only develops around age 15–16 (Baumert & Köller, 1996, ibid., p. 95).
Practice · strategies in conversation
  • Make them conscious: “How did you remember that?” Students often use good strategies without knowing it.
  • Explain the benefit: “What could the mind map be useful for?” (against the production deficiency)
  • Announce the dip: “At first the new method is slower. Give it two weeks.”
  • Try it out and evaluate: agree on a small experiment and discuss it in the next conversation.
own synthesis after Hasselhorn & Gold, 2022; Kremers & Schlüter, 2018, ch. 6
“She’s just a visual learner.”True?
The assumption of fixed learning styles is not supported by evidence. Differences in preferences are more likely habits than type-based dispositions. In conversation, it helps more to ask about specific strategies that fit the task than about a type.Hasselhorn & Gold, 2022, pp. 98–100
Connection · staying in the learning window
Whether a hint about a strategy helps depends on whether it lies within the zone of proximal development. Graduated support (asking back, a strategy hint, modeling a sub-step, solving together) is described on the page Supporting learning processes.
B · Error culture

Dealing constructively with mistakes

“Learning means making mistakes, and so does teaching, by the way.” The mistake is not the problem; the communication about it is. Where mistakes are concealed or glossed over, learning opportunities are lost (Wisniewski & Zierer, 2017, pp. 46–47).

Correction from a deficit perspective

The teacher identifies shortcomings and measures the deviations from the subject norm.

Feedback from a development perspective

The teacher reads the traces of an individual learning path and gives recommendations for further work.

Comparison after Ruf & Gallin, 2011, p. 48.

Separating the matter from the person
If a factual mistake is combined with feedback on the person, younger students in particular may get the impression that they are a bad person. It should therefore be clear which level the feedback refers to.Wisniewski & Zierer, 2017, p. 47
Hints instead of solutions
Effective feedback does not give away solutions but offers hints on how mistakes can be corrected or obstacles overcome, and allows time to apply it right away.Lötscher et al., 2023, pp. 108–109 (after Narciss, 2014; Wiliam, 2018)
Ask first
Before correcting, it is worth understanding the logic behind the solution. A wrong result can contain a clever idea.Ruf & Gallin, 2011; see Analyzing learning
Practice · a conversation about a mistake in four sentences
Wisniewski and Zierer describe how a teacher hands a notebook back to a child: she shows that she values the child, names the mistake and its cause, makes clear that it can be fixed through the child’s own effort, and says that she believes the child can do the task and is available for questions.
Example: “I can see how carefully you worked. With the fractions you added the denominators. That happens to a lot of people when they have the rule for multiplying in their head. With a common denominator you’ll get it. I’m sure you can do the next three, and I’m here if you get stuck.”after Wisniewski & Zierer, 2017, p. 47; own example
B · Explaining

Explaining effectively, in conversations, too

Learning conversations also involve explaining: a rule, a strategy, a connection. Explaining and understanding are two sides of the same coin. A correct explanation does not automatically lead to learning success, however; what matters is how students process it (Brüning & Siewert, 2026, p. 7).

1 Prior knowledge

build on it, anticipate misconceptions

2 Adaptivity

watch for understanding, ask briefly

3 Activation

stimulate thinking rather than just listening

4 Capacity

short portions, 8–12 minutes at most

5 Clarity

linguistically suitable and precise

6 Question

an explanation answers a question

7 Common thread

connect sub-steps visibly

8 Why

not only the how, but also the why

9 Illustrate

fitting, uncluttered visuals

10 Practice

without application, explaining has no effect

Ten features of effective explanations, summarized after Brüning & Siewert, 2026, pp. 8–9.

Learning by explaining
Besides learning from explanations there is learning by explaining: those who explain something themselves have to put it into words, order it and structure it. Explaining to each other is potentially more effective than passive listening, but it needs guidance and the language skills to explain and to ask.Brüning & Siewert, 2026, pp. 7, 10 (after Kollar, 2023)
“I’m not explaining that again”
According to students, whether enough is explained in class depends mainly on the relationship. Teachers who react brusquely to questions are rarely asked again. Where only right answers count, students are reluctant to ask for explanations and would rather ask AI.Brüning & Siewert, 2026, pp. 9–10
Practice · explaining in a learning conversation
  • Have them explain first: “Explain to me how you understood it.” That makes prior knowledge visible.
  • Explain briefly, then check: “What was the key step just now?”
  • Have them explain it back: the student explains the idea in their own words or with their own example.
  • Follow up with practice: have them solve a fitting task right there in the conversation.
own synthesis after Brüning & Siewert, 2026; on perspective-taking when explaining see Nückles & Hipp, 2026, on the page Learning prerequisites

C · Structure

Structuring conversations: four models compared

A good learning conversation is not pleasant chatting in which the goal gets lost (Kremers & Schlüter, 2018, p. 25). Various models divide it into phases. They differ in their terms but follow the same logic: contact and goal, current situation, options, next step.

ModelContact and goalClarifying the situationOptionsActing and securing
GROWWhitmoreGoal: topic and goalReality: what is the situation?Options: what is possible?Will: what will you do first?
LEAPTolhurstLooking at goalsExploring realityAnalysing possibilitiesPlanning action
COACHRauenCome together, OrientationAnalysisChangeHarbour: goal reached, closing

Own comparison after Kremers & Schlüter, 2018, pp. 25–27 (GROW after Whitmore, 2006; LEAP after Tolhurst, 2012; COACH after Rauen, 2008) and Bamberger, 2022, table 5.1, p. 66. The assignment of Bamberger’s phases is simplified.

Contact first
Bamberger puts synchronization at the beginning as a separate phase: getting to know each other, seeing the person in their competencies, clarifying the concern and the request. Effectiveness studies show it to be the most important success factor. A common beginner’s mistake is to rush to the solution after listening only briefly, the “trap of premature solution focus.”Bamberger, 2022, p. 66 (after Flückiger & Wüsten, 2014)
Model or dance?
Phase models offer orientation, not a script. Bamberger compares a good counseling conversation to a spontaneous dance whose steps emerge from the interplay. Sticking rigidly to a manual can weaken the relationship.Bamberger, 2022, p. 67

Case example: “The mountain of studying before the tests”

An 8th-grade student asks her subject teacher for learning coaching because all her tests suddenly seem too hard. The teacher first clarifies the setting: confidential and not graded. Goal: the student writes down the topic and goal herself: “Tips and tricks to keep an overview and memorize better.” Reality: she describes a typical study evening; the teacher sorts out what goes through her head and what she feels, and asks about solutions she has tried. Options: together they collect ideas; only now does the teacher contribute knowledge about time planning and study techniques. Will: the student formulates four rules of her own and a weekly study plan. “When and with what will you start?” – “Tonight.” shortened after Kremers & Schlüter, 2018, pp. 80–85

C · Conversation skills

Listening, asking, acknowledging, encouraging

Bamberger arranges the basic strategies of a counselor in a four-field scheme: attention is directed either at the situation or at action, and the aim is either to clarify or to expand (2022, p. 64).

focused on the situationHow is it?
focused on actionWhat to do?
clarifying
listeningactive listening, asking follow-up questions, summarizing: “Did I understand correctly that …?”
askingopen, solution-focused questions: “What have you already tried?”
expanding
acknowledgingmaking resources and efforts visible: “You stuck with it, even though …”
encouragingprompting first steps and showing confidence: “What would be a small first step?”

Own illustration after Bamberger, 2022, table 4.3, p. 64; example sentences my own.

Asking instead of telling
Instructions lead to quick reactions, but following them is not the same as learning. Questions assume that the answer lies within the person and set their own thinking in motion.Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 27–28
Holding back advice
The teacher only contributes their own ideas once the student has already named many ideas of their own and agrees. A solution that seems logical to me may not fit into their life.Kremers & Schlüter, 2018, pp. 12–13
Activating resources
Resources are abilities, interests, experiences, successes, relationships, values and much more, within the person (internal), in relationships (relational) and in the environment (external).Bamberger, 2022, pp. 49–50 (after Schär et al., 2012)
Practice · small things, big effect
  • Seating: at a corner at a 90-degree angle rather than face to face, so eye contact is possible but not forced.
  • Visualizing: write the topic, goal and ideas on cards; ideally the student writes them.
  • Small talk: short everyday conversations about interests and hobbies build relationships and reveal resources that are otherwise overlooked.
  • “Yes, and” instead of “yes, but.”
Kremers & Schlüter, 2018, p. 9; Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 25, 27
C · Solution-focused

Asking solution-focused questions

The language of problems is very different from the language of solutions. Talking about past problems usually leads to blame, defensiveness and a shrug: “I don’t know.” Talking about solutions is about the future: who will do what, when and how (Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 24–25).

Exception question

No problem is always there. “Tell me about a time when you could have gotten angry but stayed calm. How did you do that?”Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 21, 36

Scaling question

“On a scale of 1 to 10, where are you today? What helped you get to 4 already? How would you know that you’re at 5?”Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 33–35

Other people’s perspective

“Imagine I asked your best friend: how would she notice that you’re withdrawing less?”Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 31, 34

“Instead” question

Stopping something is not a skill. “What would you like to do instead?”Bauer & Hegemann, 2013, pp. 35, 75

Resource question

“How have you solved situations like this so far? Who could help you?”Kremers & Schlüter, 2018, p. 83; Bauer & Hegemann, 2013, step 5

Asking for good reasons

Instead of “Why did you …?”: “I can imagine you had a good reason. Will you tell me?”Berg & Shilts, 2005, p. 41
When the number is low
Many teachers hesitate to use scales because they fear low numbers. Stay calm and ask honestly: “What helped you get to 2?” At 1 or 0: “How come it isn’t minus 2?” Even in difficult situations, something is already working.Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 35, 37–38
Well-formed goals
Goals should be positive (“instead”), process-oriented (“how”), in the here and now (“next time”), as specific as possible (“exactly”) and within your own control (“What can you do about it?”).Bauer & Hegemann, 2013, fig. 3, p. 35 (after Walter & Peller, 2004)

Exercise: rephrasing questions

How could the question be asked in a solution-focused way? Think first, then click.

Example: Timmy is late

Timmy was late almost every morning. Talking about being late didn’t help. The teacher decided to talk to him only on the days he was on time and asked how he had managed it. Timmy told her that on the other days he looked after his little brothers when his mother had to work overtime at the hospital. Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 21–22 (shortened)
Tension · solution-focused, not blind to problems
Being solution-focused does not mean ignoring difficulties. The problem is first acknowledged (“I can understand that this isn’t easy for you”), then narrowed down: when and where does it occur, and when not? That way it no longer appears as a trait of the person but as something tied to situations.Bauer & Hegemann, 2013, pp. 34–35
C · Program

Ich schaffs: turning problems into skills

The program Ich schaffs (“I can do it”) is based on Ben Furman’s Kids’ Skills from Finland. Bauer and Hegemann adapted it for adolescents aged 13 to 17. The basic idea: learning works best with confidence, when it is worthwhile, and together with others. Instead of talking about problems, the question is: which skill do you want to learn? (2013, pp. 11–13, 72)

Finding a goal
  1. Look at life as a journey through time: what annoys you, what would you like to be different?
  2. Set a goal: what exactly do you want to learn?
  3. Look at the benefits: for you and for others
  4. Name the skill you need for it
Building support
  1. Find helpers
  2. Find a motto and a symbol
  3. Look for three reasons to be optimistic
  4. Ask your helpers why they trust you
Practicing and sticking with it
  1. Make a step-by-step plan
  2. Get going: the first small step
  3. Keep a log and show your successes
  4. Prepare for setbacks
Wrapping up
  1. Celebrate and say thank you
  2. Pass on the new skill (optional)
  3. Think about the future (optional)

The 15 steps, summarized and grouped after Bauer & Hegemann, 2013, pp. 18–21. The grouping is my own.

Example: Frederik and the presentation

Frederik, 13, is shy and very afraid of his first presentation. He loves fantasy stories. The school social worker lets him tell a story and asks: “Imagine you’re a successful storyteller and lots of people are listening to you. What would that be like?” Frederik pictures a packed stadium. Then they think together about what skills such a storyteller needs: telling from memory, looking at the audience, speaking loudly, making notes, bravely saying “I’ve forgotten that now.” shortened after Bauer & Hegemann, 2013, pp. 72–74
Vision and goal
A vision doesn’t have to be realistic, but it has to carry you: a soccer pro, or a storyteller in a stadium. Only once it motivates are concrete, achievable goals derived from it, tied to a skill that can be learned.Bauer & Hegemann, 2013, p. 35
Line of thought · in everyday school life
Not all 15 steps fit every situation. Even single elements change a conversation: asking about the skill rather than the problem, a helper from the class, a log for small successes, preparing for setbacks (own synthesis; tool 4 below).
C · Criticism

Giving critical feedback without hurting

It is easier to accept advice than criticism, easier to accept a suggestion than advice, and easier to accept a wish than a suggestion. Furman and Ahola therefore propose turning criticism into a wish for the future (2017, pp. 115–117).

1Be specificWhat exactly bothers you about the behavior, not the character?
2Look for a good explanationToo much at once? Unclear task? Worries? My share in it?
3Formulate a wishWhat should the person do instead?
4Suggestor ask what they themselves suggest
5Discuss the benefits“Would that have any other advantages?”
6Show confidence“I’m sure you can do it.”
7Plan a strategytogether, with a trial run
8Acknowledge progressand encourage others to recognize it

Own illustration of the principles after Furman & Ahola, 2017, pp. 116–123.

Basic assumption
People don’t behave inappropriately because they “are like that,” but because they don’t know, don’t understand or don’t think of how they could do it differently. Most know very well what they are doing wrong, but not how to improve it.Furman & Ahola, 2017, p. 123
Receiving criticism in six phases
Teachers also receive criticism, from students, parents or colleagues. Furman and Ahola suggest: listen, say thank you, accept the emotion, apologize for what happened, turn the criticism into hope (“What do you think I should do in the future?”) and make an agreement.Furman & Ahola, 2017, pp. 131–133
Practice · an example from middle school
Instead of: “You’re unreliable. No homework again.”
Better: “I think it would be good if we made a plan for how the assignments can be done by Thursday. What do you think? … And if it doesn’t work out once, let me know beforehand and we’ll find a solution. Shall we try this for a week and then have a quick check-in?”own example based on the approach in Furman & Ahola, 2017, pp. 118–122
“The sandwich method: praise, criticism, praise.”Does it help?
Hardly. It doesn’t help students when critical comments are softened with praise; besides, they quickly learn to read the praise as a herald of criticism. More effective is clear, matter-of-fact feedback with a concrete next step.Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 109 (after Hattie, 2014); second sentence my own addition
C · Class

WOWW: working on what already works

WOWW stands for Working On What Works. Insoo Kim Berg and Lee Shilts transferred the solution-focused approach to entire classes at a middle school in Florida, with students from special education classes and dropout prevention programs. Learning is the student’s own achievement; teaching means supporting it (2005, p. 9).

1

Observing

and reporting back at the end of the lesson what went well in the class
2

Self-assessment

The class rates itself on a scale.
3

Handover

The teacher takes over, also writing letters to students and parents.
4

Goals and scales

Goals are agreed on together and scaled.
5

Class scale

“What does our class look like at 7?”

Five implementation steps after Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 44–60.

How it went
A visitor noted 12 to 15 observations per lesson of what the class was doing well, such as “Michael asked questions politely and at the right moment,” and read them out in the last ten minutes. At first the scale ratings were between 2.5 and 4, later between 5 and 6. There was never any scolding. Weeks later, students still remembered the compliments.Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 45–49
Good goals according to WOWW
  • the presence of a solution rather than the absence of a problem
  • specific, observable as behavior
  • important to students and teacher
  • small, simple, quickly achievable
  • the beginning of a change, not its end
  • described in the social context
Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 29–32 (selection)
Practice · letters with good news
Many parents only get bad news from school. A short letter or message with one specific positive observation changes the relationship. Parent conversations begin with what is going well, without hiding where learning is still needed.Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 33, 39–40
Practice · reframing
  • lazy → relaxed, takes their time
  • nosy → curious, attentive
  • complainer → wants everyone to do it well
  • stubborn → strong-willed, has their own ideas
Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 40–41
Connection · Reteaming
Furman and Ahola also apply the solution-focused approach to groups: a team chooses a development goal, describes the ideal state, collects benefits, anticipates obstacles, looks for reasons for confidence, commits to concrete steps, prepares for setbacks, keeps a log and celebrates progress (2017, pp. 135–137). This fits class goals as well as study groups.
C · Dialogic

Dialogic feedback: travel journal and check marks

Not every learning conversation is spoken. In dialogic learning according to Ruf and Gallin, students keep a travel journal, and the teacher replies in writing with feedback. The conversation takes place in the notebook.

Core idea

what the teacher considers accessible and meaningful about a subject

Assignment

open enough for many paths, but aimed at the core of the matter

Travel journal

the personal record of the path, with approaches, detours and discoveries

Feedback

builds on the traces in the travel journal; the teacher learns from it, too

The four instruments of dialogic learning after Ruf & Gallin, 2011, pp. 6–7.

Check marks instead of grades

–not yet met
✓met: individual effort is recognizable
✓✓independent, personal achievement
✓✓✓a “breakthrough”

Check marks for smaller assignments from a semester program in an upper secondary class, after Ruf & Gallin, 2011, pp. 46–47.

Self- and external assessment
In the semester program, students regularly receive feedback. Those who don’t finish an assignment on time get a second chance. The report card grade is based on an overall assessment in which students take part with a self-assessment. Differences between self- and external assessment are clarified in conversation.Ruf & Gallin, 2011, pp. 46–48
Practice · a piece of dialogic feedback
Instead of “Mistake in problem 3,” for example: “Interesting that you made a drawing first. It shows you understood what you were looking for. Then a factor got lost in the calculation. Can you find it? Try the same drawing next time, and write down what you notice.”own example in the spirit of Ruf & Gallin, 2011
C · Progress review

Leading assessment conversations and progress reviews

At the end of a semester or school year, the teacher, the student and the parents discuss the past learning period. Winter calls for a paradigm shift: no longer announcing achievement and behavior from a position of authority, but looking at development together, hearing different perspectives and agreeing on solutions, from a culture of directives to a culture of agreements (Winter, 2015, after Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 113).

1Welcome and openingclarify setting, time and procedure
2Presenting workThe student shows selected pieces of work.
3Exchangecompare self- and external assessment
4Goals and planssupport agreement, ideally based on the child’s idea
5Outlook and goodbyedates, formalities, thanks

Procedure after Winter, 2015, p. 232, cited in Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 114; explanations my own.

Preparing students
So that students have a large share of the conversation, they prepare, for example with one piece of work they enjoyed, two pieces that show progress and one piece that was hard work. Reflection questions and sentence starters help.Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 114 (after Smit, 2009)
With the child, not about the child
Because the child is both a participant in and the subject of the conversation, these conversations are demanding. The adults should talk with the child, not about the child. Sensitive topics belong in a separate conversation without the child.Lötscher et al., 2023, pp. 115–116 (after Hauser, 2015)
Support agreement
Ideally the idea for support comes from the child, because people pursue their own goals with more interest. Important: appropriate steps, clear responsibilities, an agreed way to check success. A strength can also be the subject of the agreement.Lötscher & Schär, 2006, after Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 115
Practice · middle school with subject teachers
When several teachers teach a class, the teacher leading the conversation collects their observations beforehand. The conversation is based on selected pieces of work (portfolio), not on working through an assessment grid. If needed, it is expanded into a round table with special education, school psychology or interpreters.Lötscher et al., 2023, pp. 114, 116
Practice · supporting the Matura paper
For longer papers in upper secondary school, scheduled coaching conversations at regular intervals have proven useful: setting interim goals, reflecting on the approach, working with a learning journal, addressing difficulties early. Whoever supervises and later grades the paper clarifies these roles openly.own synthesis after Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 112; Kremers & Schlüter, 2018, p. 24
Tension · grades and feedback
When grades and content feedback are given at the same time, the feedback is often overlooked: students trust the grade and consider the comment mere politeness. So, if possible, give the feedback first and the grade later, and always make clear that a grade says something about the task, not about the person.Lötscher et al., 2023, pp. 112–113 (after Hattie & Clarke, 2019; Sacher, 2009; Wiliam, 2018)

D · Toolbox

Toolbox: learning conversations in everyday teaching

Many good learning conversations last only a few minutes. The following tools can be used without much preparation (own synthesis from the sources).

Three-minute GROW

  • “What do you want to have finished today?”
  • “Where are you right now?”
  • “What could you do now?”
  • “What will you do first?”
after Kremers & Schlüter, 2018

Scale in passing

  • have them show 1 to 5 fingers
  • “What helps you already be at 3?”
  • “What would you need for a 4?”
after Berg & Shilts, 2005

One compliment per lesson

  • describe specifically what someone did
  • say what effect it had
  • don’t combine it with criticism
after Berg & Shilts, 2005; Lötscher et al., 2023

Ask back

  • “What went well for you?”
  • “What are you unsure about?”
  • only then give feedback
after Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 110

A mistake in four sentences

  • appreciation
  • mistake and cause
  • fixable through own effort
  • confidence and help
after Wisniewski & Zierer, 2017

Good news home

  • one specific positive observation
  • as a short message to parents
  • to someone different each week
after Berg & Shilts, 2005

E · Antinomies

Tensions in learning conversations

Anyone who leads learning conversations has to weigh up between poles. Move the sliders and think about where you stand in a specific situation. Below each slider is what the sources say.

counselinggrading
support ↔ assessment

Counseling and grading should be kept as separate as possible (Kremers & Schlüter, 2018). At the same time, the same teacher gives grades (Lötscher et al., 2023). → Clarify roles openly: will this conversation be graded, what will happen with what is said?

pursuing my own goallistening openly
intention ↔ openness

Teachers almost always have a goal, but hidden intentions put people off (Juul & Jensen, 2019). → Make your intention transparent, then stay curious.

acknowledging the problemlooking at solutions
problem ↔ solution

Rushing to the solution loses the person (Bamberger, 2022). Talking about problems for too long leads to defensiveness (Berg & Shilts, 2005). → First acknowledge and narrow it down, then ask about exceptions and goals.

praisinginforming
encouragement ↔ information

Praise directed at the person has little effect on learning (Wisniewski & Zierer, 2017), compliments on specific actions strengthen the relationship and confidence (Berg & Shilts, 2005). → Acknowledge specifically and combine it with information about learning.

letting them find itexplaining and advising
coaching ↔ instruction

Their own solutions carry further (Kremers & Schlüter, 2018), but teaching without explaining is hardly conceivable (Brüning & Siewert, 2026). → First the student’s ideas, then targeted explanation if it is wanted or missing.

personalprofessionally distant
closeness ↔ distance

Personal language strengthens relationships; private matters don’t replace them (Juul & Jensen, 2019). Coaching is not therapy (Kremers & Schlüter, 2018). → Be visible as a person, know the limits of your role and refer onward when needed.

The wording of the tensions is my own synthesis of the sources cited.

Wrap-up

Key points and reflection

Seven key points

1
A learning conversation is a dialogue among unequals: the teacher is responsible for the process, the student for the content.
2
There are different formats, from brief feedback to the progress review, and each needs a clear setting.
3
Feedback on process and self-regulation is rare but especially valuable. Praise directed at the person does little for learning.
4
Self-efficacy grows through own successes, close role models, credible encouragement and interpreted feelings.
5
Ask instead of tell: exceptions, scales and “instead” questions open up a view of solutions.
6
Criticism can be phrased as a wish for the future, with benefits, confidence and a trial run.
7
Every learning conversation ends with a concrete next step the student has chosen.
Food for thought
  • Which sentence do I say to students most often in class? Which level is it on?
  • When did I last ask about an exception instead of the problem?
  • To whom could I send good news home this week?
  • Where do I pursue an intention in conversations that I don’t say out loud?
“What is your next step?”

This question belongs at the end of every learning conversation. It hands responsibility for learning back to where learning happens.
