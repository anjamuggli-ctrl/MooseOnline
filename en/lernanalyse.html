Pedagogy · Topic

Analyzing learning

What is learning, how do I recognize it, and what follows from it? Theories, concepts, tensions and options for action in practice.

What it’s about

Only those who understand how students learn can support them in a targeted way. Because learning happens in the mind, all we have are traces: approaches, results, errors, progress and statements. Analyzing learning means collecting these traces carefully, interpreting them cautiously and deriving well-matched learning opportunities from them.

This page first clarifies what learning is and how children and adolescents learn. It then shows what to look at, which methods can capture learning, where your own judgment may be biased and what follows from the analysis, including collaboration, referrals and decisions about educational pathways. You can work through it from top to bottom or jump around using the table of contents.

Introduction

Starting point: What does this result tell me?

“She got a 3.5 out of 6.”
“He just didn’t get it.”
“Everyone worked really well today.”

Statements like these sum up a lot and reveal little. A grade does not show how someone thought, and a quiet classroom does not show whether anyone learned. This page asks: What actually is learning, how can I recognize it, and how do I use what I see for the next step?

Line of thought · three questions for any analysis
1
What do I see? Approaches, results, changes over time, described as precisely as possible. → Observing
2
How can it be explained? Test several interpretations: the person, the task, the teaching, the wider context. → Assessment–support cycle
3
What follows from it? For the students, for my teaching and for working with others. → Fit

Guiding idea

Learning happens in the mind and is never directly visible. All we see are traces: actions, statements, products and changes. Analyzing learning means collecting these traces carefully, interpreting them cautiously and checking the interpretation in class. The goal is not a verdict on a person but a better-matched learning opportunity.

A · Basic understanding

Learning: a definition

Anyone who wants to analyze learning needs an idea of what learning is. Depending on my concept of learning, I look at different things: only at correct answers, or also at ways of thinking, detours and collaboration.

Learners actively construct their knowledge by interpreting perceptual experiences.Gerstenmaier & Mandl, 1995, quoted in de Boer, 2012, p. 66 (own translation)

In today’s cognitive and social constructivist understanding, learning is described by four features. The Swiss curriculum (Lehrplan 21) and the literature on competency-based assessment build on this.

Reusser, 2016, after Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 33

actively constructive

Knowledge is not transferred but built by the learners themselves.

building on prior knowledge

New things are connected with what is already there, including misconceptions.

self-regulated

Learners plan, monitor and steer their learning, more or less consciously.

social

Meanings are negotiated in exchange with others.
Learning is social
Learning is not only a construction in an individual mind. It also happens in interaction with other people and with things. What an action means is negotiated in context: a raised arm only becomes “raising your hand” at school.de Boer, 2012, pp. 66–67
Learning is also relearning
Something new often emerges only through irritation and the insight into “not being able to do it yet.” Learning therefore also includes frustrating and disappointing experiences. There is no learning that is only positive.Dietrich, 2011, and Meyer-Drawe, 2008, after de Boer, 2012, pp. 67–68
Learning means building a model
From a neurocognitive perspective, the brain builds an internal model of the world when it learns and keeps refining it. Children are not born as a “blank slate” but already bring basic structures with them.Dehaene, 2020
What this means for analysis
If learning is active, dependent on prior knowledge, self-regulated and social, looking at the final product is not enough. I also need to ask: What does the person build on? How do they go about it? Who do they learn with? Where do they get stuck? (own synthesis)
A · Cognition

How the brain learns

Educational psychology describes learning as information processing: information is taken in, transformed and organized in memory. Learning can succeed or stall at every stage (Hasselhorn & Gold, 2022, ch. 2).

Own illustration based on the information processing model in Hasselhorn & Gold, 2022, ch. 2.

Three bottlenecks
1
Attention: Only what is attended to reaches working memory. Attention also fluctuates within the same person (intra-individual variability).
2
Working memory: It can hold only a little at a time. New information “pushes in” and can displace what was there.
3
Prior knowledge: What is available in long-term memory relieves working memory and gives meaning to new information.
Hasselhorn & Gold, 2022, ch. 2
Four pillars of learning
Neuroscientist Stanislas Dehaene sums up the research in four conditions that speed up learning:
  • Attention: selecting what matters
  • Active engagement: forming and testing hypotheses yourself, not just listening
  • Error feedback: finding out where your own model is wrong
  • Consolidation: repetition, practice and sleep strengthen what has been learned
Dehaene, 2020, part 3
Connection · turning the pillars into analysis questions
When a student struggles, I can ask in turn: Was attention on what mattered? Did the person have to think for themselves, or only follow along? Did they get informative feedback on their errors? Did they have a chance to practice and consolidate? This puts the learning situation in view, not just the person (own synthesis). The five individual prerequisites of the INVO model are explained on the page Learning prerequisites.
A · Development

How children and adolescents learn

Children and adolescents do not simply learn “less well” than adults. Their learning is embedded in development: the brain is being remodeled, new life tasks arise, and peers become more important. For lower secondary school, adolescence is especially relevant.

The brain is remodeling

At the onset of puberty, the frontal lobe (prefrontal cortex) forms many new synapses once more; afterwards, little-used connections are pruned. Abilities that depend on this region keep developing well into adolescence: executive functions (planning, inhibiting impulses, directing attention) and social cognition (taking other people’s perspective).Blakemore & Choudhury, 2006

Two systems, different speeds

The system for reward and social stimuli becomes highly responsive with puberty. The system for self-control matures more slowly, into early adulthood. Especially in the presence of peers, adolescents therefore take more risks. This is not due to a lack of knowledge, so shaping the context often helps more than providing information.Steinberg, 2008

Learning needs sleep

During sleep, the brain replays what was learned and consolidates it. Enough good sleep is therefore a prerequisite for learning, not a luxury, especially in adolescence.Dehaene, 2020, ch. 10
Development as a learning task
Havighurst called the age-typical demands developmental tasks. They are learning tasks that build on one another: mastering one makes the next ones easier. Today, four clusters are described for adolescents: qualifying (knowledge, training, career), bonding (body, friendships, detaching from parents), consuming and regenerating (media, leisure, recovery) and participating (own values, having a say). At the center is the search for one’s own identity. Eschenbeck & Knauf, 2018, pp. 25–28, after Havighurst, 1953, and the Shell Youth Study, 2015
Learning is relationship
Learning at school involves more than subject knowledge. Systemic pedagogy stresses that behavior and learning should not be understood in isolation as traits of a person, but within their social relationships. Even disruptive behavior makes sense in at least one context, for example when it is the only way a child gets attention. Mosell, 2016, pp. 8–9
Practice · what this means for analysis in lower secondary school
  • Fluctuations in performance can be related to development (transition, puberty, friendships), not just to the subject.
  • Whether someone shows something in front of the class depends heavily on their peers. Also analyze in protected situations, for example in a one-on-one conversation or in writing.
  • Self-regulation is still developing. Lack of planning is first a developmental and learning issue, not a character trait.
own synthesis from Blakemore & Choudhury, 2006; Steinberg, 2008; Eschenbeck & Knauf, 2018
A · Insight

Learning is not directly visible

Learning itself cannot be observed. Only the efforts and the results, that is, the changes, are visible. That someone has learned is therefore always an interpretation by the observer (Prange, 2012, and Scholz et al., 2006, after de Boer, 2012, p. 68).

Surface and depth
For teaching, too, research distinguishes a visible surface structure (methods, forms of interaction, materials) from a deep structure (the quality of learning processes). How effective teaching is depends mainly on the deep structure, which can only be inferred indirectly from statements, actions and results.Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 34
Practice · pay attention to small steps
What is interesting are the small steps between not being able to and being able to. “He is swinging” says little; “he is swinging standing up for the first time” says a lot. Precise description makes learning visible.de Boer, 2012, p. 68
“Whoever works quietly and diligently is learning.”Is that true?
Not necessarily. Diligence and quiet belong to the surface. Whether deep processing or mere copying is going on cannot be seen. Only explanations, solution paths and errors provide clues.own synthesis after Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 34; Dehaene, 2020
“The answer is right, so she understood it.”Is that true?
A correct result does not guarantee a good performance. It may have been memorized, copied or reached with a procedure that was not understood. Conversely, a wrong result can contain a clever idea.Ruf & Gallin, 2011, part 2, ch. 4

B · Process

How students go about it

The path to a result often says more than the result itself. Observing how someone approaches a task reveals strategies, uncertainties and habits that are no longer visible in the finished product.

What successful learners do
  • They plan their learning.
  • They use effective strategies and know how, when and why to use them.
  • They are motivated to actually use these strategies.
  • They monitor their learning and adjust their approach.
Hasselhorn & Gold, 2022, ch. 2
A basic repertoire of strategies
Students need general strategies: organizing their learning (time, workspace, materials), understanding content, storing and retrieving it, directing their concentration, reading and analyzing texts, presenting and coping with tests. In addition, there are subject-specific strategies, for example for vocabulary or word problems. Keller, 2015, ch. 5

How the approach shows

I see …Possible questionshypotheses, not judgments
Someone starts right away and then crosses out a lot.Does the person plan before starting? Do they know a way to understand a task first?
Someone waits until others start and then copies.Are the instructions unclear? Is confidence in their own ability missing? Is the pace too fast?
Someone reads a text three times and knows little afterwards.Is the person only repeating instead of processing the content (highlighting, summarizing, asking questions)?
Someone explains out loud what they are doing.An opportunity: thinking aloud makes strategies audible. Where does the explanation falter?

Own examples; categories after Keller, 2015, and Hasselhorn & Gold, 2022.

Practice · encourage thinking aloud
A teacher asks a student at the board: “Please tell us what’s going on in your head while you calculate, and at some point I’ll say stop.” This makes thinking audible so it can be discussed together. Students can also explain their approach to one another.example after de Boer, 2012, p. 71
B · Product

What a result reveals: the logic of a performance

A result is more than right or wrong. Every solution follows its own logic. Understanding this logic shows where someone stands and where to connect. Ruf and Gallin speak of a singular position statement: students first show in their own way how they see something before rules and norms come into play.

Example: reading a wrong solution closely

Task: A bike costs 480 francs and is reduced by 25%. How much does it cost now?

“25% is a quarter. 480 ÷ 4 = 120. So 480 + 120 = 600 francs.”

  • What works? The percentage is correctly interpreted as a fraction, and the share is calculated correctly.
  • Where is the error? The discount is added instead of subtracted.
  • What idea might be behind it? Perhaps “percent always means adding” or a misreading of “reduced.”
  • Next step: ask, don’t correct: “Is the bike cheaper or more expensive now?”
own example
The right answer is not everything
A correct result does not guarantee a good performance, and a wrong one does not rule it out. Ruf and Gallin therefore suggest appreciating performances in two dimensions: by the subject-related result and by the quality of the student’s own path. Their teaching follows the three steps I – You – We: This is how I do it. How do you do it? This is what we agree on.Ruf & Gallin, 2011, part 1 and part 2, ch. 4 and 6
Errors as windows
Errors show how someone thinks. Tasks with high diagnostic potential, conversations about results and learning paths, and working on errors together are therefore well suited for assessment.Lötscher et al., 2023, ch. 6.2
Line of thought · four questions for any product
1
What has worked, even partly?
2
Where exactly does it differ from what was expected? Is it a careless mistake or a systematic error?
3
What idea or rule might be behind it?
4
What would be the next step? And what do I still need to ask before I’m sure?
own synthesis after Ruf & Gallin, 2011; Lötscher et al., 2023
B · Over time

Making development over time visible

Whether someone is making progress only becomes clear by comparing several points in time. The standard I apply also determines whether students notice their progress at all.

Tension
Compared with the class, Lia is “below average” every time. Compared with herself, she has learned the most this year. Both statements are true. Which one she hears shapes how she sees herself (own example).
Comparisons shape self-concept
When teachers mainly emphasize the social reference norm and neglect individual gains, the self-concept of weaker students in particular suffers. With the individual reference norm, they too can experience success. Comparisons with one’s own earlier performance usually go along with a favorable development of self-concept. A higher self-concept in turn has a positive effect on later performance.Möller & Trautwein, 2020, pp. 197, 202–203

criterion-referenced

Measured against the learning goal: What can the person already do, and what not yet?

individual

Measured against the person’s earlier performance: What has been added?

social

Measured against the group. Says little about actual ability and does not serve support.

holistic

Measured against an expert view, for complex performances with many possible solutions.

Own illustration after Lötscher et al., 2023, pp. 98–99. For an assessment to be understandable, it must be clear which reference norm applies.

Exercise: Which reference norm is behind it?

“You can now add fractions with different denominators confidently. That was our learning goal.”

Criterion-referenced: performance is measured against the learning goal.

“With a 4.8 you are above the class average.”

Social: comparison with the class. Whether the learning goal was reached remains open.

“In September you used 3 out of 10 technical terms correctly, today 8.”

Individual: comparison with the person’s own earlier performance.

“You are among the top three in your grade.”

Social: rank within a group.

“Your report meets four of the five agreed criteria. Something is still missing in the citations.”

Criterion-referenced: the criteria are the standard.

Practice · making progress visible
  • Assess the same competency several times, with comparable tasks.
  • Keep earlier work and compare it with the students: “What can you do today that you couldn’t do in the fall?”
  • Give feedback in criterion-referenced and individual terms, even where the grade compares socially.
own synthesis after Lötscher et al., 2023; Möller & Trautwein, 2020
B · Obstacles

What slows learning down

When learning stalls, there is rarely just one cause. It helps to look for obstacles on several levels, in the person and in their environment. Only a search on all levels protects against settling on one explanation too soon.

Knowledge and strategies

Gaps in prior knowledge, misconceptions, missing or unsuitable learning strategies, an overloaded working memory.Hasselhorn & Gold, 2022; Keller, 2015

Motivation and explanations

Those who attribute failure to lack of ability (internal, stable, uncontrollable) make less effort. Those who rely on effort and strategy keep going. Goals aimed at mastering something foster persistence more than goals aimed mainly at looking good.Lefrançois, 2015, pp. 335–339

Emotions and anxiety

Test anxiety shows on several levels at once: in feelings, body, thoughts, motivation, causal attributions and inner images. Those who repeatedly experience events as uncontrollable can learn helplessness and give up.Kossak, 2015, ch. 2–8 and 6.4, after Seligman, 1975

Development and life circumstances

Transitions, critical life events or everyday stress tie up energy. Whether a demand becomes a burden depends on how it is appraised and on the resources available.Eschenbeck & Knauf, 2018, pp. 34–37

Teaching and environment

Quality of teaching, learning environment and task format can help or hinder. The ICF therefore always asks about barriers in the environment, too.Tönnissen et al., 2024, p. 305; Hollenweger, 2021

Arousal: too little, too much

Performance has a medium optimum of arousal. For difficult tasks it is lower: too much pressure paralyzes, too little tension bores.Yerkes–Dodson law, after Lefrançois, 2015, p. 327
Connection · classifying learning difficulties
Whether a difficulty is temporary or lasting, limited to one subject or broad, helps to classify it. The terms learning gap, learning disorder and learning disability are explained on the page Learning prerequisites (after Hagmann-von Arx et al., 2025).
Practice · the contrast question
When someone fails in one situation, ask: Where does this person succeed? If it works in practice but not in the test, the hurdle may lie more in anxiety or time pressure than in knowledge.own synthesis after Kossak, 2015
“He’s just lazy.”Is that true?
“Lazy” is an interpretation and an attribution of a trait. Low effort can have very different causes: the expectation of failing anyway, fear of being judged, missing strategies, being overwhelmed or being underchallenged. Each explanation calls for a different response.own synthesis after Lefrançois, 2015; Kossak, 2015
B · Resources

Recognizing strengths, potential and talents

Analyzing does not mean only finding deficits. Knowing strengths makes it possible to build on them. These include interests, perseverance, social skills and support in the environment, but also special talents that are not always visible in class.

Giftedness is not the same as achievement
In research, intellectual giftedness is often defined by a cutoff, for example an IQ of 130 or higher (98th percentile). This applies to about 2% of the population. Multidimensional models such as the Munich Model of Giftedness show, however, that whether giftedness turns into achievement depends on personality traits (e.g., motivation, coping with stress) and on the environment (family, teaching, classroom climate). Preckel & Vock, 2021, ch. 1.2–1.3, pp. 30–31
Overlooked talents
In studies, teachers identify only about 45% of gifted students. At the same time, 50 to 70% of those nominated are not gifted. Checklists of characteristics hardly improve this. Boys are nominated two to three times as often as girls, although both genders are gifted equally often. Because giftedness is often equated with good grades, underachievers usually go unnoticed: students whose performance lags well behind their abilities over a long period. Preckel & Vock, 2021, pp. 123–124, 195–196

Clues in class

  • surprisingly clever questions or solution paths
  • very quick grasp, but reluctance with routine work
  • large differences between oral reasoning and written grades
own synthesis; clues, not a diagnosis

Resources and protective factors

A distinction is made between personal (e.g., self-esteem, self-efficacy, self-regulation, active coping), family and social protective factors and resources. They help people cope with stress.Eschenbeck & Knauf, 2018, pp. 35–36, after Bengel et al., 2009

The right question

Behind a diagnosis, almost everything one would need to know about a child can easily disappear. Questions such as “What is Sarah especially good at?” turn attention to possibilities for action.Hollenweger, 2021, p. 35
Practice · create opportunities to show abilities
For abilities to become visible, students need situations in which they can show them: stimulating activities, varied materials, open tasks. If you only set routine tasks, you only see routine.Buschbeck, 1985, after de Boer, 2012, p. 69

A demand is not the same as a burden
According to Lazarus and Folkman’s transactional stress model, it depends on appraisal whether a demand is experienced as a challenge or a threat, and on whether a person sees resources for coping. Besides normative developmental tasks, critical life events (e.g., parents’ separation, moving) and everyday hassles can challenge adolescents.Eschenbeck & Knauf, 2018, pp. 34–37
Connection

Practice · assess development with care
  • Development is individual. Ages are guidelines, not norms.
  • Ask instead of assuming: “What’s on your mind at the moment, outside of school?”
  • At transitions (e.g., into lower secondary school or academic high school), expect temporary dips and watch closely who needs longer.

B · Complex performances

Assessing demanding performances

Projects, presentations, texts or experiments cannot be captured as right or wrong. There are many good solutions. This is where the holistic reference norm helps: a performance is good if it stands up to the criteria of experts (Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 99). To keep this from becoming arbitrary, transparent criteria are needed.

The single-point rubric
A rubric with only one level: it describes what makes a good performance and leaves room on the left for what is still missing and on the right for what goes beyond it. The criteria are developed with the students and practiced with examples. The rubric serves further learning, not grading. It lets students answer four questions: Where am I going? Where am I now? How do I get there? How can I go beyond?Fluckiger, 2010, pp. 18–20
Tension
The holistic reference norm requires expertise. Its use by less experienced teachers is controversial. Remedies: disclose the criteria, separate observation from evaluation, involve other teachers.Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 104

B · Teaching

Does my teaching fit the learning?

Every analysis of learning is also an analysis of teaching. What students show, they show in a situation I designed. Sometimes an observation reveals more about the task than about the person.

Example: the dictation

A student teacher observes Jakob during a fill-in-the-gap dictation. Jakob first checks whether he is allowed to start writing, runs his finger along the line to hit the gap, and copies the last word from his neighbor. The precise description shows that the task demands careful listening, quick reaction, correct spelling and writing on a line all at once, at the pace of the class. It says more about the limits of the task format than about Jakob’s spelling. To see his strategies, a different format would be needed. after de Boer, 2012, pp. 73–74
Two starting points
Measures can start with the person and build up missing prerequisites directly (remedial strategy) or adapt teaching to the person’s needs (compensatory strategy). Good support considers both.Trautmann & Wischer, 2007, after Tönnissen et al., 2024, p. 307
Short-term and long-term adaptation
Short-term adjustments directly in class are called micro-adaptation, longer-term adjustments to the learning offer macro-adaptation. Both require recognizing gaps between the current and target state and their possible causes.Schrader, 2013, after Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 42
Line of thought · questions for my own lesson
  • Could students show what they can do, or did the format measure something else (speed, reading, courage)?
  • Did everyone know what the goal was and how to recognize success?
  • Did they have to think for themselves, or could they just follow along and copy?
  • Where did I collect information on their level of learning, and what did I change as a result?
own synthesis after the five strategies of formative assessment (Wiliam & Thompson, 2008, after Lötscher et al., 2023, pp. 35–36) and Dehaene, 2020

C · Model

Assess, understand, support: a cycle

Diagnostic competence is the ability to accurately assess students’ learning- and achievement-related characteristics and to correctly judge the demands of tasks. Today, the focus is less on grading and more on using assessment to guide teaching and learning support (Lötscher et al., 2023, pp. 41–42).

Own simplified illustration of the assessment–support cycle as a figure eight after Tönnissen et al., 2024, pp. 304–307.

Two typical mistakes
Teacher education showed that assessment is often skipped and support begins right away. And analysis focuses too much on the child alone, without environmental factors, participation and resources. That is why the cycle starts with a bio-psycho-social picture based on the ICF-CY, and measures always target the person and the teaching.Tönnissen et al., 2024, p. 304
Status and process
Status assessment captures the current state. Process assessment shows trajectories and makes it possible to adjust support continuously. For teaching, process assessment matters most.Tönnissen et al., 2024, p. 306

Case example: Chiara, 7th grade

After moving up to lower secondary school, Chiara seems anxious and hardly participates, although her grades used to be very good. Her homeroom teacher and the special education teacher find the situation unclear and start the assessment cycle. Question: In which situations does Chiara show socially anxious behavior? Hypothesis: The composition of the class plays a part. Targeted observations and a progress meeting show that the class is friendly, but Chiara lacks strategies for new social situations. The hypothesis is rejected. The interpretation is now: the transition is overwhelming her at the moment. Support targets Chiara (a group program in class) and everyday school life (structured activities at recess). After two months, the results are reviewed. abridged from Tönnissen et al., 2024, pp. 308–309
Connection
What is described here for special education also works on a small scale in the regular classroom: ask a question, form a hypothesis, collect information in a targeted way, interpret, act and check. The page Supporting learning processes shows what support in class looks like.
C · Method

Observing and recording

Observation is part of all educational practice. In special education assessment it is considered the supreme discipline (Tönnissen et al., 2024, p. 306). Yet it is underestimated: “Just watching a bit, how hard can that be?” writes a student teacher, and soon discovers how demanding it is (de Boer, 2012, p. 65).

An observation needs a question
Without a question, you get lost in the abundance. The question becomes more precise as knowledge grows:
1
open: “How does Jakob work in math class?”
2
subject-related: “How does Jakob add decimals in writing?”
3
social: “How does Jakob solve the task together with others?”
de Boer, 2012, pp. 71–72
Practice · getting started
  • At first, limit the observation to 20 to 30 minutes.
  • Let students know that you cannot help during this time.
  • Add notes from memory at the end and finish the record as soon as possible.
  • Several short windows say more than a single long one.
de Boer, 2012, pp. 70–71

Eight rules for an observation record

1
Note the setting: place, date, time, class, subject, starting situation.
2
Two columns: description on the left, possible interpretations on the right.
3
Start during the observation and add to it right afterwards.
4
Also describe nonverbal behavior: facial expressions, gestures, posture.
5
Precise enough for outsiders to follow what happened. Do not summarize.
6
Describe actions one by one: not “Sarah was distracted” but “Sarah looks out the window, rummages in her backpack, takes out a notebook.”
7
Phrase cautiously: “probably,” “possibly,” “seems.”
8
Make subjectivity visible: “seems to me,” “I suspect.”

Summarized after de Boer, 2012, p. 77. A template for the record is included in the tools.

“Jakob is calculating with concentration.”Is that an observation?
Not yet. “With concentration” is an assessment. An observation describes how the concentration shows: he points with his finger at the numbers he is adding, says each step half aloud and only looks up after the last column.after de Boer, 2012, p. 72

The travel diary
In dialogic learning, students record in a travel diary (Reisetagebuch) not only tasks and solutions but their own paths, with their own approach and effort. It is a personal document that the teacher has access to and that becomes part of the dialogue. The teacher responds with feedback that builds on the students’ thoughts.Ruf & Gallin, 2011, foreword and part 2
Practice · good questions in a learning conversation
  • “How did you go about it?”
  • “What was hard for you, what was easy?”
  • “How did you notice that something wasn’t right?”
  • “What would you do differently next time?”

C · Method

Making resources visible: learning stories

Many observation forms look for deficits. Learning stories from New Zealand turn this around: they ask where learning is already visible and build on it. The approach comes from early childhood education but can also be used at school (de Boer, 2012, pp. 78–80).

taking an interest

What interests the person? How can I tell?

being involved

How does involvement show?

persisting

What is the challenge? How does the person persist?

communicating

How does the person express themselves? What do they communicate?

taking responsibility

How do they contribute to the learning community and take responsibility?

The five learning dispositions after Carr, in Leu et al., 2007, quoted after de Boer, 2012, p. 78.

Example for lower secondary school

Dear Selin, last week I watched you set up the heat conduction experiment with your group. When the first attempt didn’t work, you didn’t give up but asked: “What if the metal is too thin?” You suggested a new measurement and carefully entered the values in the table. I have the impression that you are interested in questions where you can find something out yourself. Would you like to take over planning the next experiment?

own example in the format after de Boer, 2012, p. 79
What makes a learning story
  • based on observations, analyzed with the five dispositions
  • written from several perspectives: impressions of several people are compared
  • written as a letter to the child, in appreciative language
  • suggests a next step and includes the child and parents in the conversation
de Boer, 2012, pp. 78–80
Tension
Focusing on resources does not mean keeping quiet about difficulties. Precise, appreciative language that avoids labeling opens doors, even for difficult topics.de Boer, 2012, p. 80
C · Self-critique

Where judgment gets distorted

Perception does not mirror the world one to one. What I see depends on me, my state of mind, my experiences and my expectations (Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 84). From a systemic perspective: whoever observes is part of what is observed and influences it (Mosell, 2016, ch. 1.3).

Expectations have effects

Students of whom teachers expect a lot receive more and harder content, more precise and more positive feedback, more time to answer and more emotional support. These students then actually perform better (Pygmalion effect).Hofer & Haimerl, 2008, after Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 84

Typical perception errors

  • Halo effect: an overall impression colors everything.
  • Projection: reading your own traits into others.
  • Logical error: “Good at math, so good at physics.”
  • Perseveration: holding on to a first impression.
  • Context effect: in a strong class, good work looks mediocre.
Jürgens, 1998, after Lötscher et al., 2023, pp. 84–85

Unequal chances

With the same average performance, Swiss students of high or middle socioeconomic status were placed in a more demanding school track 83% of the time, students from immigrant families with low status only 52%, and boys from these families just under 30%.Kronig, 2007, after Lötscher et al., 2023, pp. 122–123
Intersectionality
Social categories such as gender, background or social class do not act separately but are intertwined. Adding them up is not enough; their interactions are what matters. The finding above shows exactly this: those who are disadvantaged most are students in whom several categories come together.Walgenbach, 2012, p. 1; application to the finding: own synthesis
Practice · reducing bias
  • Separate description from evaluation. This is considered the most important step.
  • Base judgments on concrete situations and criteria.
  • Use your own notes rather than memory.
  • Involve other teachers.
  • Look specifically for observations that contradict your picture.
Bohl, 2009, and Sacher, 2009, after Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 104; last point own addition

D · Collaboration

Working together: more eyes see more

A teacher always sees only part of the picture. Others see the same person in other subjects, situations and relationships. The more perspectives come together, the easier it is to recognize bias and test interpretations.

the students themselves

They are experts on their own learning. Self-assessments, learning conversations and learning journals bring in their perspective.Lötscher et al., 2023, ch. 5.1

colleagues

Subject and homeroom teachers, special education, speech therapy, German as a second language. Special education teachers are often in the class for a few lessons a week and can observe in a targeted way.Tönnissen et al., 2024, p. 304

the parents

They know their child in other contexts and over many years. In Switzerland, the standardized progress meeting (SSG) and, if needed, the standardized assessment procedure (SAV) provide a shared framework, both based on the ICF.Tönnissen et al., 2024, pp. 303–304
Thinking systemically
People and groups influence each other mutually, in cycles rather than simple chains of cause and effect. As in a mobile, changing one part moves all the others. Escalating cycles, for example between reproach and withdrawal, are problematic. Systemic work therefore looks for patterns and asks how they can be interrupted.Mosell, 2016, ch. 1.1
Practice · interpreting together
  • Write down observations objectively before the meeting, separate from interpretations.
  • Deliberately ask about different experiences: “Where do you see her differently?”
  • Discuss difficult cases in a peer case consultation (Mosell, 2016, ch. 10).
  • Pass on only what is needed for learning, and respect confidentiality.
own synthesis
D · Referral

When a referral is needed

Not every difficulty needs a specialist service. Many questions can be clarified in class and in exchange with colleagues. When answering a question goes beyond one’s own possibilities, interdisciplinary collaboration becomes necessary (Tönnissen et al., 2024, p. 306).

clarify in class

  • difficulty is new or limited to one topic
  • an explanation is obvious (e.g., absence, new topic)
  • adjustments show an effect after a few weeks

look at it together

  • difficulty persists despite adjustments
  • it shows in several subjects or situations
  • the situation remains unclear: discuss with special education and the homeroom teacher, observe in a targeted way

suggest a referral

  • clear distress for the person, the class or the teacher
  • suspected learning disorder, disability or psychological strain
  • discuss the next steps together with the parents

Own synthesis as a guide, not a binding rule; after Tönnissen et al., 2024, and Hagmann-von Arx et al., 2025.

Who does what?
In Switzerland, the school psychology service carries out the standardized assessment procedure. Intelligence tests are administered only by psychologists. Special education teachers then have the task of using these results for support.Tönnissen et al., 2024, pp. 306–307
Connection · procedures in Graubünden
How referral to the school psychology service, accommodations and adapted learning goals are regulated in Graubünden is explained on the page Learning prerequisites: recognizing learning difficulties and taking action.
“With a diagnosis, we’ll finally know what to do.”Is that true?
Only partly. A diagnosis classifies, but says little about what helps in class. Behind a diagnosis, almost everything one would need to know about a child can disappear. That is why every assessment must be followed by close analysis in everyday school life.Hollenweger, 2021, p. 35; Tönnissen et al., 2024, p. 306
D · Decisions

Transitions and placement

For track placement and transition decisions, the prognostic function of assessment comes into play: How likely is success at a certain level, school or training program? The aim is the best possible fit between the person’s competency profile and the future demands (Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 40).

Tension · grades seem objective
Grades are condensed quality judgments, not precise measurements. Under selection pressure, however, countable points seem especially reliable and unassailable. In addition, the same grade can mean different things at different levels. Training companies therefore often pay more attention to the level attended, the application or the trial apprenticeship than to grades.Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 123
The small fish in a big pond
Students who move into a high-achieving group compare themselves with stronger peers. With the same achievement, students in strong classes usually have a lower self-concept than in weak ones (big-fish-little-pond effect). Moving up to a higher level can therefore temporarily affect self-image.Möller & Trautwein, 2020, pp. 197–198
Practice · justifying decisions well
  • Use several sources: learning progress over time, products, observations, self-assessment, other teachers’ views.
  • Give the trajectory more weight than a snapshot.
  • Check your own judgment for possible bias (gender, background, language, behavior).
  • Include the adolescents’ interests and developmental tasks: choosing a career is itself a developmental task.
  • Explain decisions transparently and keep them reversible where possible.
own synthesis after Lötscher et al., 2023, ch. 9; Eschenbeck & Knauf, 2018
D · Documentation and reflection

Documenting learning paths and reflecting on one’s own learning

What is not recorded is lost, and with it the chance to show progress. Documentation serves two purposes: it helps the teacher recognize development, and it helps students reflect on their own learning. At academic high schools in particular, this ability is an educational goal.

Collect

A portfolio or learning folder with selected work, drafts and revisions, plus the teacher’s notes with date and situation.

Assess

Students assess their own work against criteria, for example with a single-point rubric, and back up their assessment with evidence (Fluckiger, 2010).

Reflect

Learning journal or travel diary: What did I try? What worked? What will I do next? (Ruf & Gallin, 2011)
Students as owners of their learning
Only students themselves can learn. That is why formative assessment includes making them responsible for their learning. In the Swiss curriculum (Lehrplan 21), this appears under self-reflection and independence. Research speaks of metacognition or self-regulated learning.Lötscher et al., 2023, pp. 67–68
Practice · looking at one’s own learning like a researcher
Students can approach their learning like a small research project: form a hypothesis (“I remember vocabulary better with flashcards than by reading”), try out a strategy for two weeks, check the result and draw a conclusion. This links reflection with scientific thinking.own synthesis

E · Antinomies

Tensions in analyzing learning

Analyzing learning means weighing up between poles. Move the sliders and consider where you stand in a specific situation. Below each slider is what the sources say.

look closelydon’t monitor
Attention ↔ control

Whoever observes influences what happens (Mosell, 2016). Students should know when and why they are being observed (de Boer, 2012). → make observations transparent and involve students.

name difficultiesemphasize strengths
Deficit ↔ resource

Many procedures look for deficits; learning stories look for resources (de Boer, 2012). Good support needs both. → build on strengths, name difficulties precisely and without labeling.

the personthe teaching
Where is the cause?

Assessment often focuses too much on the child (Tönnissen et al., 2024). Observations sometimes reveal the limits of a task instead (de Boer, 2012). → always ask, too: What did the learning situation contribute?

act quicklyclarify carefully
Pressure to act ↔ assessment

Assessment is often skipped and support starts right away (Tönnissen et al., 2024). In class, however, much has to be decided on the spot (micro-adaptation; Lötscher et al., 2023). → act in the moment, but treat the interpretation as a hypothesis and check it later.

supportselect
individual ↔ social reference norm

School has to make placement decisions and at the same time foster development (Lötscher et al., 2023). Social comparisons weaken the self-concept of weaker students (Möller & Trautwein, 2020). → disclose reference norms, report progress even where selection takes place.

trust your own judgmentdistrust your own judgment
Expertise ↔ bias

Experienced teachers often judge accurately, but every judgment is prone to error (Lötscher et al., 2023). Teachers identify only some of the gifted students (Preckel & Vock, 2021). → justify judgments, compare them with criteria and with colleagues.

The wording of the tensions is my own synthesis of the sources cited.

Conclusion

Key points and reflection

Seven key points

1
Learning is active, dependent on prior knowledge, self-regulated and social, and it is embedded in development.
2
Learning is not directly visible. I see traces and interpret them.
3
The path often says more than the result. Every solution has its own logic.
4
Progress only becomes visible over time. The reference norm shapes students’ self-image.
5
Obstacles lie on several levels, including the teaching. Strengths matter as much as difficulties.
6
Good analysis follows a cycle: ask, collect, interpret, act, check.
7
Judgments can be biased. Describe before evaluating, get several perspectives.
Food for thought
  • Which student result did I last read only as right or wrong? What would its own logic have revealed?
  • Whose progress do I notice easily, whose hardly at all?
  • Which observation recently told me more about my task than about the students?
  • Who could I write a learning story for?
“What was this person thinking?”

This question is worth asking about every result. It keeps curiosity about students’ thinking alive and protects against hasty judgments.
