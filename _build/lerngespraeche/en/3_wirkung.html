B · Motivation

Building self-efficacy: “I can do this”

Self-efficacy is the belief that one can carry out the actions needed to reach a goal (Bandura). It is among the best predictors of later educational success at the secondary level, and it is reciprocally related to performance: good performance strengthens self-efficacy, and high self-efficacy contributes to good performance (Lefrançois, 2015, p. 339).

Four sources according to Bandura
  • Own successes: only have an effect if they are attributed to something one can influence, not to luck or an easy task.
  • Role models: comparisons with peers are the most useful.
  • Encouragement: it is easier to maintain self-efficacy when others who matter to us express their confidence in our abilities.
  • Feelings: nervousness can be interpreted as an obstacle or as energy.
Lefrançois, 2015, pp. 340–341 (after Bandura, 1986, 1997)
Explanations that help
It is favorable when success is attributed to one’s own effort or strategy, and failure to a lack of effort or to the difficulty of the task. Explanations such as talent or chance are unfavorable because they cannot be changed. Teachers therefore pay attention to sentences like “I can’t do it anyway.”Lötscher et al., 2023, pp. 108–109 (after Dweck, 2010)
Connection · self-concept and comparison
Whether someone believes they can do something also depends on what they compare themselves with. Feedback based on their own progress (individual reference norm) particularly strengthens weaker students. More on the page Analyzing learning.Möller & Trautwein, 2020, pp. 202–203
Practice · using the four sources in conversation
Make successes visible: “Show me a task you couldn’t do a month ago. What did you do to get there?”
Choose close role models: “Who in your class also found this hard at one point? How did she learn it?”
Give reasons for your confidence: “I think you can do this because last week you stuck with the equation, too.”
Reframe nervousness: “Your pounding heart shows that this matters to you. What helps you use that energy?”
own examples for the sources after Lefrançois, 2015; on expressing confidence see also Furman & Ahola, 2017, p. 116, and Bauer & Hegemann, 2013, steps 7–8
B · Learning strategies

Discussing learning strategies in conversation

Learning strategies are activities aimed at a learning or retention goal that go beyond the bare minimum (Hasselhorn & Gold, 2022, p. 89). A learning conversation is a good place to make them conscious, because many students use strategies without thinking about them, or they know a strategy and still don’t use it.

cognitive

rehearsing and memorizing, organizing (sorting, summarizing), elaborating (linking to prior knowledge, creating your own examples)

metacognitive

planning, monitoring, checking your own understanding, adjusting your approach

resource management

organizing the learning environment, time, workspace, materials and study groups

Own illustration after Hasselhorn & Gold, 2022, pp. 89–90. Keller, 2015, ch. 5, describes a basic repertoire of general strategies (see Learning prerequisites).

The motivation dip

Own illustration after Hasselhorn & Gold, 2022, fig. 2.8, p. 97 (after Miller & Seier, 1994).

How strategies are acquired
1
Mediation deficiency: the strategy cannot be used even after it has been demonstrated (mainly in very young children).
2
Production deficiency: the strategy is only used when prompted and soon abandoned because the benefit is not convincing.
3
Utilization deficiency: the strategy is used spontaneously but does not yet pay off because it is not automatic.
Hasselhorn & Gold, 2022, pp. 95–97. A repertoire that can be used flexibly often only develops around age 15–16 (Baumert & Köller, 1996, ibid., p. 95).
Practice · strategies in conversation
  • Make them conscious: “How did you remember that?” Students often use good strategies without knowing it.
  • Explain the benefit: “What could the mind map be useful for?” (against the production deficiency)
  • Announce the dip: “At first the new method is slower. Give it two weeks.”
  • Try it out and evaluate: agree on a small experiment and discuss it in the next conversation.
own synthesis after Hasselhorn & Gold, 2022; Kremers & Schlüter, 2018, ch. 6
“She’s just a visual learner.”True?
The assumption of fixed learning styles is not supported by evidence. Differences in preferences are more likely habits than type-based dispositions. In conversation, it helps more to ask about specific strategies that fit the task than about a type.Hasselhorn & Gold, 2022, pp. 98–100
Connection · staying in the learning window
Whether a hint about a strategy helps depends on whether it lies within the zone of proximal development. Graduated support (asking back, a strategy hint, modeling a sub-step, solving together) is described on the page Supporting learning processes.
B · Error culture

Dealing constructively with mistakes

“Learning means making mistakes, and so does teaching, by the way.” The mistake is not the problem; the communication about it is. Where mistakes are concealed or glossed over, learning opportunities are lost (Wisniewski & Zierer, 2017, pp. 46–47).

Correction from a deficit perspective

The teacher identifies shortcomings and measures the deviations from the subject norm.

Feedback from a development perspective

The teacher reads the traces of an individual learning path and gives recommendations for further work.

Comparison after Ruf & Gallin, 2011, p. 48.

Separating the matter from the person
If a factual mistake is combined with feedback on the person, younger students in particular may get the impression that they are a bad person. It should therefore be clear which level the feedback refers to.Wisniewski & Zierer, 2017, p. 47
Hints instead of solutions
Effective feedback does not give away solutions but offers hints on how mistakes can be corrected or obstacles overcome, and allows time to apply it right away.Lötscher et al., 2023, pp. 108–109 (after Narciss, 2014; Wiliam, 2018)
Ask first
Before correcting, it is worth understanding the logic behind the solution. A wrong result can contain a clever idea.Ruf & Gallin, 2011; see Analyzing learning
Practice · a conversation about a mistake in four sentences
Wisniewski and Zierer describe how a teacher hands a notebook back to a child: she shows that she values the child, names the mistake and its cause, makes clear that it can be fixed through the child’s own effort, and says that she believes the child can do the task and is available for questions.
Example: “I can see how carefully you worked. With the fractions you added the denominators. That happens to a lot of people when they have the rule for multiplying in their head. With a common denominator you’ll get it. I’m sure you can do the next three, and I’m here if you get stuck.”after Wisniewski & Zierer, 2017, p. 47; own example
B · Explaining

Explaining effectively, in conversations, too

Learning conversations also involve explaining: a rule, a strategy, a connection. Explaining and understanding are two sides of the same coin. A correct explanation does not automatically lead to learning success, however; what matters is how students process it (Brüning & Siewert, 2026, p. 7).

1 Prior knowledge

build on it, anticipate misconceptions

2 Adaptivity

watch for understanding, ask briefly

3 Activation

stimulate thinking rather than just listening

4 Capacity

short portions, 8–12 minutes at most

5 Clarity

linguistically suitable and precise

6 Question

an explanation answers a question

7 Common thread

connect sub-steps visibly

8 Why

not only the how, but also the why

9 Illustrate

fitting, uncluttered visuals

10 Practice

without application, explaining has no effect

Ten features of effective explanations, summarized after Brüning & Siewert, 2026, pp. 8–9.

Learning by explaining
Besides learning from explanations there is learning by explaining: those who explain something themselves have to put it into words, order it and structure it. Explaining to each other is potentially more effective than passive listening, but it needs guidance and the language skills to explain and to ask.Brüning & Siewert, 2026, pp. 7, 10 (after Kollar, 2023)
“I’m not explaining that again”
According to students, whether enough is explained in class depends mainly on the relationship. Teachers who react brusquely to questions are rarely asked again. Where only right answers count, students are reluctant to ask for explanations and would rather ask AI.Brüning & Siewert, 2026, pp. 9–10
Practice · explaining in a learning conversation
  • Have them explain first: “Explain to me how you understood it.” That makes prior knowledge visible.
  • Explain briefly, then check: “What was the key step just now?”
  • Have them explain it back: the student explains the idea in their own words or with their own example.
  • Follow up with practice: have them solve a fitting task right there in the conversation.
own synthesis after Brüning & Siewert, 2026; on perspective-taking when explaining see Nückles & Hipp, 2026, on the page Learning prerequisites
