A · Basic understanding

Conversation, dialogue, relationship

Not every conversation at school is a dialogue. Often the point is to pass on information as accurately as possible. A learning conversation aims for more: the participants should come to understand something together that neither of them fully knew beforehand, namely how this person learns and how things can move forward.

Communication

Information is passed from one person to another as accurately as possible, such as instructions or a date. In the classroom this is often necessary and useful.

Dialogue

Both listen to each other freely and without prejudice and in doing so create something new that both make their own. Neither tries to sell the other their own truth.

Distinction by David Bohm, cited in Juul & Jensen, 2019, p. 147.

Unequal roles
The relationship between teacher and students is asymmetrical: the teacher has more power and more responsibility. Equality is therefore not possible, but equal dignity is. The adult is responsible for the quality of the conversation.Juul & Jensen, 2019, pp. 146–148
Who is responsible for what?
In coaching, the teacher is responsible for the process (setting, structure, questions, appreciation), the student for the content (topic, goal, solution, next step).Kremers & Schlüter, 2018, pp. 9–10
Conversation and relationship
Every conversation has a content aspect and a relationship aspect (Watzlawick). Juul and Jensen put it this way: the quality of relationships rises with the quality of conversations.Bamberger, 2022, p. 69; Juul & Jensen, 2019, p. 149
Tension · your own intention
Teachers almost always pursue a goal in conversations that goes beyond the conversation itself. That is legitimate. It becomes problematic when the intention stays hidden (“You sense the intention and you are put off,” Goethe). Juul and Jensen advise stating your intention openly while staying curious about the other person’s view:
“I’d like to talk with you, Thomas, because I don’t like the way you treat the others. But I don’t know how you see it. That’s why I hope we can talk about it.” – “I’m only mean because they always tease me …” – “They tease you? I didn’t realize that. Can you tell me more?”Juul & Jensen, 2019, pp. 149–150 (example shortened)
A · Conversation formats

From feedback in passing to the progress review

Learning conversations differ in length, occasion, participants and scope. Lötscher, Naas and Roos describe three levels at which assessments are communicated: in class, in conversations with students and parents, and at the institutional level in the report card (2023, pp. 106–107).

Not all counseling is the same
  • Expert counseling (mentoring): an experienced person gives advice and support.
  • Coaching (process counseling): the counselor helps sharpen the person’s own goals and develop their own solutions. Advice comes at the very end, if at all, and only on request.
  • Learning coaching: a special form in which knowledge about learning and study techniques may also be offered.
  • Limit: coaching is not psychotherapy.
Kremers & Schlüter, 2018, pp. 12–13, 16–20, 80–81
Line of thought · clarifying roles
The same teacher counsels, teaches and grades. Kremers and Schlüter recommend keeping counseling and grading as separate as possible (p. 24). Where that is not possible, transparency helps: say at the beginning whether the conversation will be graded and what will happen with what is said. Juul and Jensen also distinguish conversations that are mandatory, spontaneous, planned or necessary (2019, p. 146). The clearer the setting, the more open the conversation (own synthesis).

A · Levels

What are we talking about? Four levels of feedback

Feedback is among the most effective factors for learning success; in Hattie’s synthesis it reaches an effect size of d = 0.75 (Wisniewski & Zierer, 2017, p. 25). What matters, however, is not the amount but the quality, and part of that is the question of which level the feedback addresses (pp. 26–31, after Hattie & Timperley, 2007).

Task

How well was the task done and understood? What is right, what is wrong?

Process

How was the work done? Which strategies, which careless mistakes, what pace?

Self-regulation

How did the person steer their learning: attention, time, monitoring?

Self

Evaluation of the person: “You’re awesome!”, “You’re a hard-working student.”

Which feedback occurs in class

Own illustration of the ranges from three studies (Hattie & Masters, 2011; Van den Bergh et al., 2010; Gan, 2011), after Wisniewski & Zierer, 2017, p. 33.

Finding
Students get the feedback they need most least often: feedback on self-regulation makes up only about 1–2% of feedback in class. When teachers are asked which feedback they themselves would like after a classroom observation, the vast majority choose exactly this level.Wisniewski & Zierer, 2017, pp. 32–33
The “self” level
Praise and blame directed at the person contain no information about learning and have little effect, sometimes even a negative one: those who are praised don’t want to risk their positive image, and frequent praise creates dependence. For the relationship, feedback on the person can still be valuable. The guiding principle is “less is more.”Wisniewski & Zierer, 2017, pp. 29–31; Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 109
Novices and advanced learners
Those new to a field mainly need feedback on the task: how is a child supposed to know that 3 + 6 = 8 is wrong? Those who already know a lot usually know themselves what went wrong and need help with self-regulation. Best for everyone is complete feedback that connects the three learning levels.Wisniewski & Zierer, 2017, pp. 34–36
Three directions
Feed back: How am I doing, what has been added since last time?
Feed up: Where am I going, where do I stand compared with the goal?
Feed forward: What is the next step?
In a conversation these questions are not worked through rigidly but used like a spotlight, depending on the situation.Lötscher et al., 2023, pp. 110–111; Wisniewski & Zierer, 2017, pp. 36–38

A · Ways of speaking

Five ways of speaking and personal language

Juul and Jensen distinguish five ways of speaking. Each has its place. For relationships and for conversations about personal concerns, however, only one works: personal language (2019, pp. 151–157).

social

polite, general; regulates distance, ideal for small talk

academic

analyzes, forms hypotheses, aims for objectivity

professional

technical terms with a clear meaning; provides certainty

pedagogical

judging, suggestive, advising; from matter-of-fact to manipulative

personal

your own thoughts, values and feelings, authentic: “I would like …”, “I think …”
Personal is not private
Personal language shows where I stand as a person on something. Private stories from my own life are something else and do not replace personal contact. An “I” at the beginning of a sentence does not make a judgment personal: “I really can’t see that you’re interested” is still a judgment. And words are only credible when body language and voice match them.Juul & Jensen, 2019, pp. 155–158
Practice · helping students find personal language
Adolescents often play down serious concerns. Adults can offer them words:
  • “You look like you don’t agree. Is that right?”
  • “I’d rather have an honest no than a half-hearted yes.”
Juul & Jensen, 2019, pp. 151, 161

A · Stance

Equal in dignity, appreciative, solution-focused

Conversation techniques only work if the stance behind them is right. Three sources describe this stance from different directions but reach similar conclusions.

Equal dignity

Everyone deserves equal respect, even with different status. The views, needs, feelings and self-images of everyone involved have the same priority. No one is dismissed because their feelings are considered “childish” or “unrealistic.”Juul & Jensen, 2019, pp. 171–172

Recognition rather than praise

Recognition means accepting a child’s inner reality and self-image as equally valid, with openness and curiosity. Praise and criticism strengthen self-confidence (“What can I do?”), recognition strengthens self-esteem (“Who am I?”).Juul & Jensen, 2019, pp. 74–77, 175–177

Solution focus

Students are experts on their own lives and have resources. Problems are normal and not always present. A solution means doing more of what works and trying something else when something doesn’t work.de Shazer & Dolan, 2008, after Bamberger, 2022, pp. 11–12
Assumptions “until proven otherwise”
The WOWW approach assumes that all students want their parents and teachers to be proud of them, that they want to learn new things, make choices, say what they think and belong to a group. It also assumes that teachers want to be good teachers and parents want to be good parents. Such assumptions show in the language we use.Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 15–16
Connection · praise under the microscope
Three sources, one direction: praise directed at the person has hardly any effect on learning (Wisniewski & Zierer), it can encourage unfavorable attributions (Lötscher et al.), and it does not replace recognition (Juul & Jensen). More effective are specific compliments about what someone has done, as used in the WOWW approach (Berg & Shilts, 2005, p. 32), combined with information on the task, process or self-regulation (own synthesis).
Practice · turning praise into information
  • instead of “Great!” → “You simplified the equation first. That made the rest much easier.”
  • instead of “You’re so talented.” → “You tried three ways until one worked.”
  • instead of “Well done!” → “I was impressed by how calmly you explained it to the class.”
own examples after Lötscher et al., 2023, pp. 109–111
