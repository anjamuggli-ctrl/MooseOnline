C · Structure

Structuring conversations: four models compared

A good learning conversation is not pleasant chatting in which the goal gets lost (Kremers & Schlüter, 2018, p. 25). Various models divide it into phases. They differ in their terms but follow the same logic: contact and goal, current situation, options, next step.

Own comparison after Kremers & Schlüter, 2018, pp. 25–27 (GROW after Whitmore, 2006; LEAP after Tolhurst, 2012; COACH after Rauen, 2008) and Bamberger, 2022, table 5.1, p. 66. The assignment of Bamberger’s phases is simplified.

Contact first
Bamberger puts synchronization at the beginning as a separate phase: getting to know each other, seeing the person in their competencies, clarifying the concern and the request. Effectiveness studies show it to be the most important success factor. A common beginner’s mistake is to rush to the solution after listening only briefly, the “trap of premature solution focus.”Bamberger, 2022, p. 66 (after Flückiger & Wüsten, 2014)
Model or dance?
Phase models offer orientation, not a script. Bamberger compares a good counseling conversation to a spontaneous dance whose steps emerge from the interplay. Sticking rigidly to a manual can weaken the relationship.Bamberger, 2022, p. 67

Case example: “The mountain of studying before the tests”

An 8th-grade student asks her subject teacher for learning coaching because all her tests suddenly seem too hard. The teacher first clarifies the setting: confidential and not graded. Goal: the student writes down the topic and goal herself: “Tips and tricks to keep an overview and memorize better.” Reality: she describes a typical study evening; the teacher sorts out what goes through her head and what she feels, and asks about solutions she has tried. Options: together they collect ideas; only now does the teacher contribute knowledge about time planning and study techniques. Will: the student formulates four rules of her own and a weekly study plan. “When and with what will you start?” – “Tonight.” shortened after Kremers & Schlüter, 2018, pp. 80–85

C · Conversation skills

Listening, asking, acknowledging, encouraging

Bamberger arranges the basic strategies of a counselor in a four-field scheme: attention is directed either at the situation or at action, and the aim is either to clarify or to expand (2022, p. 64).

Asking instead of telling
Instructions lead to quick reactions, but following them is not the same as learning. Questions assume that the answer lies within the person and set their own thinking in motion.Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 27–28
Holding back advice
The teacher only contributes their own ideas once the student has already named many ideas of their own and agrees. A solution that seems logical to me may not fit into their life.Kremers & Schlüter, 2018, pp. 12–13
Activating resources
Resources are abilities, interests, experiences, successes, relationships, values and much more, within the person (internal), in relationships (relational) and in the environment (external).Bamberger, 2022, pp. 49–50 (after Schär et al., 2012)
Practice · small things, big effect
  • Seating: at a corner at a 90-degree angle rather than face to face, so eye contact is possible but not forced.
  • Visualizing: write the topic, goal and ideas on cards; ideally the student writes them.
  • Small talk: short everyday conversations about interests and hobbies build relationships and reveal resources that are otherwise overlooked.
  • “Yes, and” instead of “yes, but.”
Kremers & Schlüter, 2018, p. 9; Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 25, 27
C · Solution-focused

Asking solution-focused questions

The language of problems is very different from the language of solutions. Talking about past problems usually leads to blame, defensiveness and a shrug: “I don’t know.” Talking about solutions is about the future: who will do what, when and how (Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 24–25).

Exception question

No problem is always there. “Tell me about a time when you could have gotten angry but stayed calm. How did you do that?”Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 21, 36

Scaling question

“On a scale of 1 to 10, where are you today? What helped you get to 4 already? How would you know that you’re at 5?”Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 33–35

Other people’s perspective

“Imagine I asked your best friend: how would she notice that you’re withdrawing less?”Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 31, 34

“Instead” question

Stopping something is not a skill. “What would you like to do instead?”Bauer & Hegemann, 2013, pp. 35, 75

Resource question

“How have you solved situations like this so far? Who could help you?”Kremers & Schlüter, 2018, p. 83; Bauer & Hegemann, 2013, step 5

Asking for good reasons

Instead of “Why did you …?”: “I can imagine you had a good reason. Will you tell me?”Berg & Shilts, 2005, p. 41
When the number is low
Many teachers hesitate to use scales because they fear low numbers. Stay calm and ask honestly: “What helped you get to 2?” At 1 or 0: “How come it isn’t minus 2?” Even in difficult situations, something is already working.Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 35, 37–38
Well-formed goals
Goals should be positive (“instead”), process-oriented (“how”), in the here and now (“next time”), as specific as possible (“exactly”) and within your own control (“What can you do about it?”).Bauer & Hegemann, 2013, fig. 3, p. 35 (after Walter & Peller, 2004)

Exercise: rephrasing questions

How could the question be asked in a solution-focused way? Think first, then click.

Example: Timmy is late

Timmy was late almost every morning. Talking about being late didn’t help. The teacher decided to talk to him only on the days he was on time and asked how he had managed it. Timmy told her that on the other days he looked after his little brothers when his mother had to work overtime at the hospital. Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 21–22 (shortened)
Tension · solution-focused, not blind to problems
Being solution-focused does not mean ignoring difficulties. The problem is first acknowledged (“I can understand that this isn’t easy for you”), then narrowed down: when and where does it occur, and when not? That way it no longer appears as a trait of the person but as something tied to situations.Bauer & Hegemann, 2013, pp. 34–35
C · Program

Ich schaffs: turning problems into skills

The program Ich schaffs (“I can do it”) is based on Ben Furman’s Kids’ Skills from Finland. Bauer and Hegemann adapted it for adolescents aged 13 to 17. The basic idea: learning works best with confidence, when it is worthwhile, and together with others. Instead of talking about problems, the question is: which skill do you want to learn? (2013, pp. 11–13, 72)

Finding a goal
  1. Look at life as a journey through time: what annoys you, what would you like to be different?
  2. Set a goal: what exactly do you want to learn?
  3. Look at the benefits: for you and for others
  4. Name the skill you need for it
Building support
  1. Find helpers
  2. Find a motto and a symbol
  3. Look for three reasons to be optimistic
  4. Ask your helpers why they trust you
Practicing and sticking with it
  1. Make a step-by-step plan
  2. Get going: the first small step
  3. Keep a log and show your successes
  4. Prepare for setbacks
Wrapping up
  1. Celebrate and say thank you
  2. Pass on the new skill (optional)
  3. Think about the future (optional)

The 15 steps, summarized and grouped after Bauer & Hegemann, 2013, pp. 18–21. The grouping is my own.

Example: Frederik and the presentation

Frederik, 13, is shy and very afraid of his first presentation. He loves fantasy stories. The school social worker lets him tell a story and asks: “Imagine you’re a successful storyteller and lots of people are listening to you. What would that be like?” Frederik pictures a packed stadium. Then they think together about what skills such a storyteller needs: telling from memory, looking at the audience, speaking loudly, making notes, bravely saying “I’ve forgotten that now.” shortened after Bauer & Hegemann, 2013, pp. 72–74
Vision and goal
A vision doesn’t have to be realistic, but it has to carry you: a soccer pro, or a storyteller in a stadium. Only once it motivates are concrete, achievable goals derived from it, tied to a skill that can be learned.Bauer & Hegemann, 2013, p. 35
Line of thought · in everyday school life
Not all 15 steps fit every situation. Even single elements change a conversation: asking about the skill rather than the problem, a helper from the class, a log for small successes, preparing for setbacks (own synthesis; tool 4 below).
C · Criticism

Giving critical feedback without hurting

It is easier to accept advice than criticism, easier to accept a suggestion than advice, and easier to accept a wish than a suggestion. Furman and Ahola therefore propose turning criticism into a wish for the future (2017, pp. 115–117).

1Be specificWhat exactly bothers you about the behavior, not the character?
2Look for a good explanationToo much at once? Unclear task? Worries? My share in it?
3Formulate a wishWhat should the person do instead?
4Suggestor ask what they themselves suggest
5Discuss the benefits“Would that have any other advantages?”
6Show confidence“I’m sure you can do it.”
7Plan a strategytogether, with a trial run
8Acknowledge progressand encourage others to recognize it

Basic assumption
People don’t behave inappropriately because they “are like that,” but because they don’t know, don’t understand or don’t think of how they could do it differently. Most know very well what they are doing wrong, but not how to improve it.Furman & Ahola, 2017, p. 123
Receiving criticism in six phases
Teachers also receive criticism, from students, parents or colleagues. Furman and Ahola suggest: listen, say thank you, accept the emotion, apologize for what happened, turn the criticism into hope (“What do you think I should do in the future?”) and make an agreement.Furman & Ahola, 2017, pp. 131–133
Practice · an example from middle school
Instead of: “You’re unreliable. No homework again.”
Better: “I think it would be good if we made a plan for how the assignments can be done by Thursday. What do you think? … And if it doesn’t work out once, let me know beforehand and we’ll find a solution. Shall we try this for a week and then have a quick check-in?”own example based on the approach in Furman & Ahola, 2017, pp. 118–122
“The sandwich method: praise, criticism, praise.”Does it help?
Hardly. It doesn’t help students when critical comments are softened with praise; besides, they quickly learn to read the praise as a herald of criticism. More effective is clear, matter-of-fact feedback with a concrete next step.Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 109 (after Hattie, 2014); second sentence my own addition
C · Class

WOWW: working on what already works

WOWW stands for Working On What Works. Insoo Kim Berg and Lee Shilts transferred the solution-focused approach to entire classes at a middle school in Florida, with students from special education classes and dropout prevention programs. Learning is the student’s own achievement; teaching means supporting it (2005, p. 9).

1

Observing

and reporting back at the end of the lesson what went well in the class
2

Self-assessment

The class rates itself on a scale.
3

Handover

The teacher takes over, also writing letters to students and parents.
4

Goals and scales

Goals are agreed on together and scaled.
5

Class scale

“What does our class look like at 7?”

Five implementation steps after Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 44–60.

How it went
A visitor noted 12 to 15 observations per lesson of what the class was doing well, such as “Michael asked questions politely and at the right moment,” and read them out in the last ten minutes. At first the scale ratings were between 2.5 and 4, later between 5 and 6. There was never any scolding. Weeks later, students still remembered the compliments.Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 45–49
Good goals according to WOWW
  • the presence of a solution rather than the absence of a problem
  • specific, observable as behavior
  • important to students and teacher
  • small, simple, quickly achievable
  • the beginning of a change, not its end
  • described in the social context
Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 29–32 (selection)
Practice · letters with good news
Many parents only get bad news from school. A short letter or message with one specific positive observation changes the relationship. Parent conversations begin with what is going well, without hiding where learning is still needed.Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 33, 39–40
Practice · reframing
  • lazy → relaxed, takes their time
  • nosy → curious, attentive
  • complainer → wants everyone to do it well
  • stubborn → strong-willed, has their own ideas
Berg & Shilts, 2005, pp. 40–41
Connection · Reteaming
Furman and Ahola also apply the solution-focused approach to groups: a team chooses a development goal, describes the ideal state, collects benefits, anticipates obstacles, looks for reasons for confidence, commits to concrete steps, prepares for setbacks, keeps a log and celebrates progress (2017, pp. 135–137). This fits class goals as well as study groups.
C · Dialogic

Dialogic feedback: travel journal and check marks

Not every learning conversation is spoken. In dialogic learning according to Ruf and Gallin, students keep a travel journal, and the teacher replies in writing with feedback. The conversation takes place in the notebook.

Core idea

what the teacher considers accessible and meaningful about a subject

Assignment

open enough for many paths, but aimed at the core of the matter

Travel journal

the personal record of the path, with approaches, detours and discoveries

Feedback

builds on the traces in the travel journal; the teacher learns from it, too

The four instruments of dialogic learning after Ruf & Gallin, 2011, pp. 6–7.

Check marks instead of grades

–not yet met
✓met: individual effort is recognizable
✓✓independent, personal achievement
✓✓✓a “breakthrough”

Check marks for smaller assignments from a semester program in an upper secondary class, after Ruf & Gallin, 2011, pp. 46–47.

Self- and external assessment
In the semester program, students regularly receive feedback. Those who don’t finish an assignment on time get a second chance. The report card grade is based on an overall assessment in which students take part with a self-assessment. Differences between self- and external assessment are clarified in conversation.Ruf & Gallin, 2011, pp. 46–48
Practice · a piece of dialogic feedback
Instead of “Mistake in problem 3,” for example: “Interesting that you made a drawing first. It shows you understood what you were looking for. Then a factor got lost in the calculation. Can you find it? Try the same drawing next time, and write down what you notice.”own example in the spirit of Ruf & Gallin, 2011
C · Progress review

Leading assessment conversations and progress reviews

At the end of a semester or school year, the teacher, the student and the parents discuss the past learning period. Winter calls for a paradigm shift: no longer announcing achievement and behavior from a position of authority, but looking at development together, hearing different perspectives and agreeing on solutions, from a culture of directives to a culture of agreements (Winter, 2015, after Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 113).

1Welcome and openingclarify setting, time and procedure
2Presenting workThe student shows selected pieces of work.
3Exchangecompare self- and external assessment
4Goals and planssupport agreement, ideally based on the child’s idea
5Outlook and goodbyedates, formalities, thanks

Procedure after Winter, 2015, p. 232, cited in Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 114; explanations my own.

Preparing students
So that students have a large share of the conversation, they prepare, for example with one piece of work they enjoyed, two pieces that show progress and one piece that was hard work. Reflection questions and sentence starters help.Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 114 (after Smit, 2009)
With the child, not about the child
Because the child is both a participant in and the subject of the conversation, these conversations are demanding. The adults should talk with the child, not about the child. Sensitive topics belong in a separate conversation without the child.Lötscher et al., 2023, pp. 115–116 (after Hauser, 2015)
Support agreement
Ideally the idea for support comes from the child, because people pursue their own goals with more interest. Important: appropriate steps, clear responsibilities, an agreed way to check success. A strength can also be the subject of the agreement.Lötscher & Schär, 2006, after Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 115
Practice · middle school with subject teachers
When several teachers teach a class, the teacher leading the conversation collects their observations beforehand. The conversation is based on selected pieces of work (portfolio), not on working through an assessment grid. If needed, it is expanded into a round table with special education, school psychology or interpreters.Lötscher et al., 2023, pp. 114, 116
Practice · supporting the Matura paper
For longer papers in upper secondary school, scheduled coaching conversations at regular intervals have proven useful: setting interim goals, reflecting on the approach, working with a learning journal, addressing difficulties early. Whoever supervises and later grades the paper clarifies these roles openly.own synthesis after Lötscher et al., 2023, p. 112; Kremers & Schlüter, 2018, p. 24
Tension · grades and feedback
When grades and content feedback are given at the same time, the feedback is often overlooked: students trust the grade and consider the comment mere politeness. So, if possible, give the feedback first and the grade later, and always make clear that a grade says something about the task, not about the person.Lötscher et al., 2023, pp. 112–113 (after Hattie & Clarke, 2019; Sacher, 2009; Wiliam, 2018)
