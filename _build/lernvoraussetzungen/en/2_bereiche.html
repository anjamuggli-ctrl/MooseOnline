B · Prior knowledge

Prior knowledge: the most important starting point

What prior knowledge does
Prior knowledge improves the quality of information processing:
1
It helps to decide what is relevant and thus directs selective attention.
2
It relieves working memory because concepts are activated more quickly.
3
It makes it easier to use learning strategies and regulate them metacognitively.
Hasselhorn & Gold, 2022, pp. 87–88
Line of thought
Working memory is a “bottleneck.” Extensive, well-connected prior knowledge can widen it (p. 125). That is why time spent activating and assessing prior knowledge is almost always worthwhile, especially with learners who quickly seem overwhelmed.
Subject teachers often overestimate students’ prior knowledge.Nückles & Hipp, 2026, p. 35
Three guidelines for explanations that support learning
1
Adapting to prior knowledge: build on what learners already know. Otherwise, an explanation seems too abstract to learners with little prior knowledge and redundant to those with a lot.
2
Cohesion: connect statements linguistically and in content (“therefore,” “in contrast”), use technical terms consistently, keep a common thread.
3
Focus on principles: explain not only the “how” but also the “why.”
Nückles & Hipp, 2026, pp. 34–36
Practice · taking the learners’ perspective before explaining
Professional explaining requires deliberately taking the learners’ perspective (Nückles & Hipp, 2026, p. 35): Which terms do the learners already know? Which examples are familiar to them? Where might misunderstandings arise? Whether an explanation supported learning shows in how well it was understood, so it is worth asking for feedback on it (p. 36). The subject matter analysis helps here: if you work through the content yourself in a very practical way, you will recognize possible stumbling blocks (Marti & Camenisch, 2018, Sachanalyse, p. 1).

B · Motivation, volition, emotion

Wanting, sticking with it, feeling

Motivation and self-concept
Dispositional characteristics include the degree of hope for success or fear of failure. Typical causal attributions after success and failure and the self-concept of one’s own ability are also significant.Hasselhorn & Gold, 2022, p. 125

How do I explain a failure to myself?

Own illustration of Weiner’s classification scheme, cf. Hasselhorn & Gold, 2022, pp. 109–110.

Line of thought
After failure, attributing it to lack of ability (internal, stable) is particularly unfavorable: those who think this way see little reason to try again. Feedback that points to effort and strategy keeps the way open.
Volition: from wishing to doing
When wishes become intentions, a person moves from motivational deliberation to volitional implementation, which mobilizes energy for persistent goal pursuit (Heckhausen’s Rubicon model). Volitional characteristics such as action or state orientation influence whether learning intentions are carried out.Hasselhorn & Gold, 2022, pp. 125–126
Learning-related emotions
Emotions can promote or hinder the activation of cognitive and motivational mechanisms. If information processing is impaired, e.g., by test anxiety, volitional control can offset this.Hasselhorn & Gold, 2022, pp. 125–126
B · Development

Developmental tasks: what else adolescents are dealing with

School is not the only task in adolescents’ lives. Havighurst’s concept of developmental tasks describes demands that typically arise in certain stages of life and build on one another (Eschenbeck & Knauf, 2018, p. 25).

Qualifying

acquiring and applying knowledge, school and vocational education, financial independence

Bonding

body and gender identity, emotional detachment from parents, friendships, partnership

Consuming and recovering

dealing with consumer goods, media and leisure activities, recovery

Participating

own system of values and norms, political participation

Clusters of developmental tasks based on the Shell Youth Study (2015), presented in Eschenbeck & Knauf, 2018, p. 27. Own illustration.

When does something become stressful?

Own illustration of the transactional model of stress by Lazarus & Folkman, in Eschenbeck & Knauf, 2018, pp. 37–38.

Appraisal is decisive
Whether someone experiences a demand as a threat or a challenge depends largely on how they assess their own coping resources.Eschenbeck & Knauf, 2018, pp. 37–38
Protective factors
personal: positive self-concept, self-efficacy, self-regulation, social competence
family: secure attachment to at least one parent, positive family climate
social: support, adults as role models, prosocial friendships, good school quality and an appreciative school climateEschenbeck & Knauf, 2018, Table 2.3, p. 36 (based on Bengel et al., 2009)
Line of thought
When a student seems absent in class, it may have to do with a completely different developmental task: a break with her group of friends, choosing a career, her own body. Developmental tasks are learning prerequisites that cannot be seen.
Practice
School itself is a protective factor. Fair and consistent rules, appropriate performance expectations and respectful, interested interaction by teachers are considered features of good school quality (Eschenbeck & Knauf, 2018, p. 36). In addition to behavior-oriented measures (e.g., strengthening life skills), environment-oriented measures are needed, i.e., a supportive environment (p. 46).
B · Attachment and relationships

Attachment experiences shape how learning happens

Attachment theory links early relationship experiences with later social-emotional health and school success. Securely attached learners, for example, are more curious, explore more extensively and can tolerate uncertainty better (Jungmann & Reichenbach, 2020, pp. 126–127).

The learning triangle: learner, teacher, task

secure (B)

balanced: the teacher is a secure base from which the task is explored; help is actively sought.

insecure-avoidant (A)

focus on the task, the teacher is avoided; rarely seeks help, feelings are suppressed.

insecure-ambivalent (C)

focus on the relationship with the teacher; the task is ignored as a disruption, risk of falling short of one’s potential.

disorganized (D)

small group with the most severe behavioral problems; patterns hard to recognize, features of all types.

Own illustration based on the learning triangle by Geddes (2007), in Jungmann & Reichenbach, 2020, pp. 127–135.

Understanding instead of labeling
The learning triangle is an interpretive offer, not a diagnostic tool for teachers. It helps to understand seemingly irrational behavior: a boy who tears up a warm-up puzzle and leaves the room may not be “cheeky” but overwhelmed by a situation without a clear task (case study based on Geddes, p. 128).
Limits of pedagogical action
With insecure attachment types, pedagogical interventions can achieve a great deal. With attachment disorders, they are necessary but not sufficient; therapeutic support in a multidisciplinary team is then needed.Jungmann & Reichenbach, 2020, p. 26
Practice · sensitivity
Observe signals carefully: Is the person interested and engaged? How do they communicate? Which strategies do they use when they have difficulties? Are the needs of quiet children recognized as well? And: Do I provide enough structure and, at the same time, enough room?Jungmann & Reichenbach, 2020, p. 157 (areas for reflection)
B · Learning difficulties

Learning difficulties, learning disorder, learning disability: a “word salad”

Hagmann-von Arx, Hengartner & Lütolf (2025) bring order to the terminology. Learning difficulties serves as an umbrella term for all forms of impaired development of school performance. They affect about 20 to 25% of all children and adolescents (pp. 3–4, based on Gold, 2018; Mähler, 2021). The German terms used in Switzerland do not map one-to-one onto English ones; the translations below are approximations.

generalseveral or almost all subjects
temporarytransient
Learning gaps in individual subjectse.g., after changing schools or an accident
School difficultiese.g., after critical life events
lastingpersistent
Learning disorder / learning weaknessreading and spelling disorder (dyslexia), math disorder (dyscalculia) or weakness
general learning weakness (learning disability)to be distinguished from: intellectual disability

Own illustration based on Hagmann-von Arx et al., 2025, Table 1, pp. 3–4 (adapted from Klauer & Lauth, 1997).

Disorder or weakness?
For a learning disorder according to ICD-10/11, in addition to considerable underachievement (simple discrepancy criterion), there must also be a discrepancy with intelligence (double discrepancy criterion). If this is not met, it is called a learning weakness. However, research shows hardly any differences: errors, course and benefit of support are comparable. The criterion is therefore heavily criticized; the DSM-5 does without it.Hagmann-von Arx et al., 2025, pp. 5–9 (based on Mähler, 2021)
Measures can reinforce inequality
Accommodations (compensation for disadvantages) are perceived more positively than adapted learning goals, which tend to be associated with stigmatization and negative consequences for motivation and self-esteem. Children from families with low socioeconomic status receive accommodations less often but adapted learning goals more often.Hagmann-von Arx et al., 2025, p. 13 (based on Sahli Lozano et al., 2020, 2023)
Line of thought · Who has the difficulty?
Kossmann (2019) describes “learning disability” as a term rooted in school organization: it exists partly because some children need more support than the school can provide with regular resources. It is one-sided to attribute deficits exclusively to the learners (quoted in Hagmann-von Arx et al., 2025, p. 13).
Practice · ask precisely
When you hear “She has a learning weakness,” it is worth asking: In which area? Is it about reading and spelling, math or several areas? The authors explicitly recommend this clarification and avoid the term “learning disability” in conversations with parents (pp. 9–10, 14).
B · Learning difficulties in the classroom

Recognizing learning difficulties and taking action

Teachers do not make diagnoses. But they are often the first to notice something, and they can provide support right away. Five questions guide this section: How does it show? What else could it be? What do I do? How is it diagnosed? What support is available?

1 · How do learning difficulties show?

Reading

reads noticeably slowly and/or inaccurately; understands what is read much less well than what is heard; avoids reading

Writing

many mistakes, even with common words; the same word spelled differently in the same text; difficulty with grammar, punctuation, text structure

Math

mistakes and a lot of time needed for basic skills (understanding numbers and quantities) and basic arithmetic; difficulty with word problems

Individual subjects

pronounced, persistent difficulties, e.g., in foreign languages or sciences, despite practice

Learning in general

behind in several or almost all subjects; needs considerably more repetition and help than the class

Accompanying signs

test or school anxiety, avoidance, withdrawal, disruptive behavior, attention problems: learning and behavioral difficulties are often closely linked

Own synthesis based on the German S3 guideline on dyscalculia (DGKJP, 2018, p. 6: underachievement in accuracy and processing time; frequent accompanying symptoms such as math, test or school anxiety), the core areas of diagnosing reading and spelling disorders (reading accuracy, speed, comprehension, spelling; LMU Klinikum, information on dyslexia) and Hagmann-von Arx et al. (2025, pp. 5–6, 12).

Line of thought · a single sign is not yet a finding
What matters is the pattern over time: Does the difficulty persist despite practice and adjustments? Does it show in one area or everywhere? That is why the first step is to observe descriptively (tool 2 in the toolbox).

2 · What else could it be?

Distinguishing
  • A sudden change after changing schools, a separation or illness tends to point to temporary difficulties (Hagmann-von Arx et al., 2025, p. 4).
  • German as a new language: it is not the migration background itself but a lack of language proficiency or other risk factors that make learning harder; this is a learning disadvantage, not a learning disorder (p. 16).
  • Vision, hearing and health are always checked as part of an assessment (LMU Klinikum, information on dyslexia).
  • Fit: What does the task implicitly require (reading, strategies, pace)?
“With Elif, it’s a learning weakness.”Is that true?
Someone who is just learning German needs language support. In the canton of Graubünden, support lessons for speakers of other languages are provided by the school authority and are not a special education measure; special education support should not be used prematurely.AVS GR, Richtlinien Sonderpädagogische Massnahmen, 2025, p. 21
“He has a learning weakness.”What exactly?
Ask: Is it about reading and spelling, math or several areas in the sense of a general learning weakness?Hagmann-von Arx et al., 2025, pp. 9–10
“I can only do something once there’s a diagnosis.”Is that true?
No. Differentiated instruction, adaptive explaining, teaching strategies and integrative support without adapting learning goals are possible without a diagnosis. Accommodations also require that existing support measures such as differentiated instruction are used first.AVS GR, Richtlinien zum Nachteilsausgleich, 2013

3 · What do I do if I suspect something? The process in Graubünden

1

Observe, document

descriptively, over several weeks and situations
2

Adapt teaching

differentiate, grade forms of help, observe the effect
3

4

Conversation

with the learner and with the parents
5

6

Own illustration based on AVS Graubünden: Richtlinien Sonderpädagogische Massnahmen (2025, pp. 7–19) and the guide of the school psychology service (2024).

Who is responsible?
The homeroom teacher has overall responsibility for learners with special educational needs and shares it with the special education teacher (SHP). Together with the SHP, they prepare the review meeting. The school authority or principal decides on low-threshold measures, the cantonal office on high-threshold ones. Measures are usually reviewed every six months.AVS GR, Richtlinien SPM, 2025, pp. 7, 14–19
Referral to the school psychology service
First, the teacher uses the expert resources at the school and implements initial support measures. The referral is made jointly by the teacher and the parents or guardians; their consent is required. Teachers can also consult the SPD without a referral.AVS GR, Leitfaden SPD, 2024

4 · How is it diagnosed?

Who and how?
In Graubünden, diagnoses are made by the SPD (learning disorders), speech therapy (language disorders) or physicians (visual, hearing and physical disabilities). The SPD aims to understand the child as a whole: interviews, observation, testing, play and school visits; strengths, weaknesses and resources, including in the child’s environment.AVS GR, Richtlinien zum Nachteilsausgleich, 2013; Leitfaden SPD, 2024
Criteria
A learning disorder requires considerable underachievement in a standardized achievement test (usually percentile rank < 7) and an IQ ≥ 70. According to ICD-10/11, there must also be a clear discrepancy with intelligence (Mia: PR 5, IQ 100). If this is missing (Lea: PR 5, IQ 80), it is called a learning weakness; according to DSM-5, it would still be a learning disorder.Hagmann-von Arx et al., 2025, pp. 5–8
Criticism of the double criterion
Children with a learning disorder and a learning weakness hardly differ in their errors, course and information processing, and they benefit equally from support. Around 43% of children with considerable underachievement do not meet the second criterion: about 10% of all students, who often receive less attention despite their learning difficulties. Some school psychology services therefore diagnose according to DSM-5.Hagmann-von Arx et al., 2025, pp. 8–9 (based on Mähler, 2021; Fischbach et al., 2013)
Three areas of criteria
The S3 guideline on dyscalculia bases the diagnosis on psychometric, clinical and qualitative criteria. If clinical and qualitative criteria support the suspicion, a deviation of one standard deviation (PR ≤ 16) is sufficient. It advises against the intelligence discrepancy criterion.DGKJP, 2018, p. 6

5 · What support is available?

Without a diagnosis: what I can do right away

  • Differentiate: grade tasks, time, approaches and help (differentiated instruction)
  • Explain adaptively: build on prior knowledge, make connections visible, explain the “why” (prior knowledge)
  • Teach learning strategies deliberately (learning strategies)
  • Lower barriers that are not the learning goal, e.g., reading the task aloud, word banks, more time in class
  • Attribute failures to changeable causes (motivation)
  • Integrative support for prevention or without adapting learning goals, after a review meeting at the round table
  • German as a second language or support lessons for speakers of other languages

With a diagnosis or expert report (Graubünden)

  • Accommodations (Nachteilsausgleich): same learning goals, different format, e.g., more time for tests, computer instead of handwriting, spelling not included in grading, oral instead of written, separate room. Prerequisites: diagnosis (SPD, medical or speech therapy) and an expert report; agreement at the round table; annual review; no note on the report card required.
  • Integrative support with adapted learning goals (individual learning goals): only with a school psychology report; an individual support plan is mandatory.
  • Pedagogical-therapeutic measures such as speech therapy or psychomotor therapy.
  • High-threshold measures such as integrative special schooling: application via the SPD, decision by the cantonal office.

AVS Graubünden: Richtlinien zum Nachteilsausgleich (2013); Richtlinien Sonderpädagogische Massnahmen (2025, pp. 5–16). Cantonal regulations change; the current version can be found on gr.ch (AVS, ordinances and guidelines, in German).

Tension · measure and stigma
Accommodations are considered more conducive to equal opportunities; adapted learning goals tend to be associated with stigmatization and negative consequences for motivation and self-esteem. Children from families with low socioeconomic status receive accommodations less often but adapted learning goals more often. Transparent communication and regular review are therefore essential.Hagmann-von Arx et al., 2025, pp. 13–14 (based on Sahli Lozano et al., 2020, 2023)
Practice · support takes perseverance
Support targets the identified focus areas and, where appropriate, should use evidence-based, disorder-specific programs. Accommodations do not replace support. Tool 3 in the toolbox helps you prepare for the round table meeting.DGKJP, 2018, pp. 6–7; AVS GR, 2013
B · Disability

Disability: characteristic, attribution or barrier?

How disability is understood helps determine what is done pedagogically. The consequences for action differ greatly depending on the understanding (Sturm, 2024, p. 116).

Four paradigms: where does the cause lie?

person-centered (medical model): disability as the consequence of an impairment, a “personal fate.”
interactionist: disability as an attribution by the environment (labeling approach), arising in the situation.
systems-theoretical: disability as a consequence of the system, e.g., through the differentiation of the school system by age and performance.
societal: disability as a consequence of social conditions and structures.

Own illustration based on Scholz, 2010, pp. 51–53 (based on Bleidick; Müller; Cloerkes).

Medical, social, cultural
Sturm (2024) distinguishes an essentializing understanding (disability as impairment and an individual characteristic), a social model (distinction between impairment and disability; the cause lies in social barriers) and a cultural understanding (even the distinction “normal/impaired” is culturally produced). The WHO’s ICF describes disability as an interaction of body functions, activities, participation, and environmental and personal factors.Sturm, 2024, pp. 114–118
Two case studies
Karl uses a wheelchair. On a mountain hike on a path that is not wheelchair-accessible, his participation is hindered; on a visit to an accessible museum, it is not. The teacher’s choice of destination shapes the environment Karl encounters.
Maja does not manage to follow the conversation rules in the morning circle. Here, the disability lies in the interaction situation: competencies are assumed that she has not yet been able to acquire.Sturm, 2024, pp. 118–119
Guiding question
Not just “What can’t this person do?” but: What support does this person need to acquire and demonstrate what is expected? (based on Sturm, 2024, p. 119)
B · The group

The class as a group: it, too, has a stage of development

Learning prerequisites do not only exist in individuals. A group is also at a certain point in its development. Stahl (2017) extends Tuckman’s (1965) phase model into a cycle.

Own illustration based on Stahl, 2017, pp. 75–79 and Table 4.1.

Forming

getting to know, assessing and placing each other. Securing a sense of belonging.

Storming

showing and standing up for oneself. Differences and conflicting goals become visible.

Norming

committing, agreeing. Rules and a “group contract” emerge.

Performing

contributing, getting involved. Working on the basis of the agreements.

Re-Forming

taking stock, reorienting: “Do the old rules still apply?”

based on Stahl, 2017, pp. 76–79

Line of thought
A newly formed class in September is in the forming stage: open group work with a lot of negotiation may be overwhelming at this point. After the fall break, storming issues may surface. The group’s stage is a learning prerequisite for choosing forms of social interaction in class.
Disruptions are social phenomena
Disruptions are not static characteristics of a person: even “hyperactive” learners have calm phases. What matters is how those involved react to one another. Disruptions can come from learners and from the teacher.Wettstein & Scherzinger, 2022, p. 34
Practice · classroom culture
The teacher helps shape the group: they lead the class, build a positive classroom culture and protect weaker members. Without this, peer norms may develop in which disrupting is considered “cool.” Changing such a culture again takes a lot of energy.Wettstein & Scherzinger, 2022, pp. 86–87
