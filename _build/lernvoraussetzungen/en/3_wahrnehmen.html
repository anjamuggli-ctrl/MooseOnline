C · Social perception

The first impression and what distorts it

Teachers form a picture of learners very quickly. That is unavoidable and often useful. Social psychology shows, however, that our impressions can be systematically distorted (Werth, Denzler & Mayer, 2020, ch. 5).

Halo effect

One striking characteristic colors the overall judgment. In a classic experiment, essays were graded better when a photo of an attractive person was attached (Werth et al., 2020, sec. 5.1.1).

Similarity and familiarity

People who are similar to us or seem familiar tend to be judged more positively (pp. 144–149).

Correspondence bias

We tend to explain other people’s behavior through their person (“he’s lazy”) rather than through the situation (“the task was unclear”), also called the fundamental attribution error (pp. 155–168).

Availability and context

Whatever is currently present (a remark in the staff room, the previous lesson) influences how we interpret new information (pp. 169–174).

Expectations

Expectations guide what we see and can fulfill themselves through our own behavior (self-fulfilling prophecy; pp. 174–176; Wettstein & Scherzinger, 2022, ch. 4).

First impression

It forms very quickly and creates an expectation that guides further perception (Werth et al., 2020, p. 140). When observing, too, what comes first or last often carries more weight (position effect; Fisseni, 2004, pp. 132–134).

Line of thought
Under time pressure, we fall back on quick, automatic judgments. Yet teachers almost always act under time pressure (Wettstein & Scherzinger, 2022). All the more important are routines that slow down judgment: describe before you interpret; ask before you act.
Practice · three counter-questions
  • What situational explanation would also be possible?
  • How does the person behave in other subjects, groups, situations (consistency, distinctiveness)?
  • What have I seen, and what have I merely heard?
C · Prejudice and stereotypes

Prejudice: why it is so persistent

Stereotypes are simplified, generalizing ideas about groups (“pictures in our heads,” Lippmann). Prejudices combine them with an evaluation. They are not simply errors of thinking but serve functions for the person (Thomas, 2006).

Six functions
Orientation in a confusing world · adaptation to the norms of one’s own group · defense against insecurity and self-doubt · positive self-presentation · demarcation and identity of the in-group · steering and justifying behavior toward the out-group.Thomas, 2006
In-group and out-group
Even an arbitrary division into groups is enough for people to favor their own group (minimal group experiments after Tajfel). Stereotypes are also activated automatically and are resistant to change, because contradicting information is easily dismissed as an “exception.”Thomas, 2006
Connection
Categories such as “migration background,” “educationally disadvantaged” or “ADHD” are sometimes necessary for planning. At the same time, they activate stereotypes. That is one reason why the analysis of learning conditions should be worded factually, free of prejudice and value judgments (Marti & Camenisch, 2018).
Practice
Intercultural learning is more likely to succeed when you become aware of your own cultural imprint, notice differences without judging them prematurely, and reflect on experiences together (Thomas, 2006, Fig. 3, paraphrased). For the classroom: get to know individuals, not groups.
C · Observing

Observing means: describe first, interpret later

Every observation already contains interpretation. Fisseni (2004) therefore recommends starting with concrete descriptions and forming abstract interpretations only later. Observe first, then evaluate.

1 Describe

“Lia looks at her phone three times during the explanation.”

2 Interpret

“Maybe she’s distracted, maybe she’s waiting for a message.”

3 Evaluate

“That’s disruptive.” Only here, and deliberately.

Exercise: description or interpretation?

Decide for each statement. The answer appears immediately.

“Noah is unmotivated.”

Interpretation. A description would be, e.g.: “Noah has not started a task in the last three lessons.”

“Sara raises her hand twice during the lesson and asks a question about task 3.”

Description: concrete, observable, without judgment.

“Elias is aggressive.”

Interpretation (even a trait judgment). What exactly did Elias do, when, toward whom?

“Mira hands in the worksheet after 20 minutes with task 1 solved and task 2 blank.”

Description. Possible interpretations (time, understanding, motivation) come only afterwards and remain hypotheses.

“The class is restless today because vacation starts tomorrow.”

Interpretation with a causal attribution. What exactly was “restless”? And is the cause correct?

Typical observation errors
  • Halo effect: one characteristic outshines the others (Asch: “warm” vs. “cold”)
  • Contrast and similarity errors: judging in comparison with oneself
  • Position effect: what comes first and last carries more weight
  • Expectation effect: you see what you expect
  • Leniency, severity, central tendency when rating
  • Overload: too many characteristics at once, unclear definitions, an unfamiliar group
Fisseni, 2004, pp. 132–134
Practice · planning an observation
  • What am I observing? A few clearly defined characteristics.
  • When and for how long? Several short windows instead of one overall impression.
  • How do I record it? Keywords with time and situation, judgment-free language.
  • With whom do I compare notes? A second view from the special education teacher, a colleague or the person themselves.

D · Building on

Building on prerequisites: subject matter analysis and learning structure

Subject matter analysis as a bridge
Working through the content yourself gives you an overview, reveals stumbling blocks and lets you react more flexibly to questions. The teacher’s subject competence provides confidence and makes helpful feedback possible. However, the empirical relationship with learning success is weaker than often assumed (based on Meyer; Hattie).Marti & Camenisch, 2018, Sachanalyse
Line of thought
The analysis of learning conditions and the subject matter analysis belong together: one asks “Who is learning?”, the other “What is difficult about the content?”. Only where the two meet does fit become plannable.

D · Differentiated instruction

Differentiated instruction: by what do I differentiate?

Kress (2019) distinguishes several variants of differentiated instruction. Each has strengths, and almost every one has a downside.

by level

Tasks at different levels of difficulty (e.g., after Bloom). Caution: When learners may choose, they usually follow their self-concept or their grade in the subject, not their content-related abilities.

by learning pace

Different amounts of time. Caution: Different paces have different causes. More time (or more tasks) alone often does not solve the problem.

by inclination

Choosing topics or products by interest. Strengthens autonomy and motivation.

by mode of access

Different approaches (text, image, hands-on). Caution: A guided assignment in which the teacher decides who learns best through which approach is not possible; understanding processes are too complex for that.

Own illustration based on Kress, 2019, ch. 3.

Line of thought · four children, four reasons
Kress describes four learners whose pace does not match the class: Anke works very precisely and gets lost in details, Lora is a “chaotic thinker,” Michael is easily distracted, and Sergej always finishes immediately but seems unengaged. More time does not help the first three, more tasks do not help Sergej. The same observation, “pace,” four different learning prerequisites. This is exactly what careful observation is for.
Guided and natural
In guided differentiation, the teacher decides who works on what. In natural differentiation, an open task offers everyone access at their own level. The two can be combined.Kress, 2019, chs. 2–3
D · Disruptions

Disruptions as signals of learning prerequisites

Classroom disruptions are disruptions of the teaching-learning process. Wettstein & Scherzinger (2022) understand them interactionally: they arise in the interplay of teacher, learners and situation. According to the supply-use model, both sides share responsibility.

Understand

What does the behavior tell me? Being overwhelmed, being underchallenged, a missing strategy, a conflict in the group, a relationship issue?

Prevent

Solid relationships (ch. 9) and clear, proactive classroom management (ch. 10) prevent many disruptions.

Include yourself

What part does my teaching play? How do I react, and what pressure am I under right now?

“He always disrupts.”Is that true?
Disruptions are not static characteristics. Even learners who disrupt a lot have calm phases. So the question is: When, during what, with whom does he not disrupt? The key often lies there.Wettstein & Scherzinger, 2022, p. 34
“Teachers and learners see the same thing.”Is that true?
Teachers and learners perceive disruptions differently and evaluate them differently. What the teacher experiences as a disruption may, for learners, be a reaction to an unclear or unfair situation.Wettstein & Scherzinger, 2022
“With enough experience, you no longer get it wrong.”Is that true?
First impressions, categories and expectations also affect experienced teachers. Those who know them are more likely to check them (ch. 4; cf. social perception).Wettstein & Scherzinger, 2022, ch. 4
